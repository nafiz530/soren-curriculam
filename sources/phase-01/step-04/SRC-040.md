# SRC-040 — Al Amin & Greenwood, The Examination System in Bangladesh and Its Impact

**Title:** “The examination system in Bangladesh and its impact: on curriculum, students, teachers and society”  
**Authors:** Md Al Amin and Janinka Greenwood  
**Journal:** *Language Testing in Asia*, 8, article 4  
**Publication date:** 2018-03-27  
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1186/s40468-018-0060-9  
**Type:** Peer-reviewed open-access mixed-methods study  
**Status:** Referenced; full publisher text reviewed for methods and findings

## Research design

The wider investigation began with a survey of 216 secondary-school English teachers from different regions of Bangladesh. The article also reports a qualitative investigation using interviews with students (n=42), teachers (n=35), teacher trainers (n=12), principals (n=4), parents (n=12), and other related professionals (n=15), as well as observations and analysis of curriculum documents and media accounts. The qualitative participants were recruited using a snowball approach. The article focuses on English language teaching and the role of examinations within the wider social system.

## Reported findings and argument

The authors report tensions between teachers' understanding of curriculum expectations and their described practices. Survey respondents described pressure from head teachers, parents, and students to secure high grades; some reported omitting textbook content they considered less likely to appear on examinations. Qualitative accounts describe how SSC/HSC results affect progression, social standing, school and teacher reputations, and the private coaching industry. The authors argue that the examination system is both a means of assessment and part of a wider social process that sustains its influence.

## Use in this project

This is a central Bangladesh-specific source for a multi-mechanism account of exam pressure. It links curriculum–test alignment, teacher practice, family expectations, institutional reputation, coaching, and social allocation. It also offers a counterweight to blaming teachers alone: teachers, parents, students, schools, and policymakers may be responding to the same incentives.

## Appraisal and limitations

- The survey measures teacher attitudes and reported practices; it is not a direct standardized measure of student learning or a national causal estimate.
- The qualitative sample is broad but snowball-recruited; the authors explicitly present narratives as illustrative rather than representative of every learner.
- The study is focused on English language teaching and is from 2018. It cannot establish the current situation across all subjects or after later curriculum changes.
- The authors' structural interpretation is an argued synthesis, not an experimentally identified causal model.
- The article does not prove that examinations have no valid certification or selection role, nor that any specific alternative would be fairer.
