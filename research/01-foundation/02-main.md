# Step 2 — What Is Education?

**Author:** Md. Nafiz  
**Status:** Initial research draft — review pending  
**Official research title:** *Is our education system actually preparing students for the future, or is it mainly preparing students to pass examinations?*

> This chapter begins the investigation; it does not establish a final definition of education. I will revise it as the source base grows and competing interpretations are examined.

## 1. Why the definition matters

Before asking whether an education system prepares students for the future, I need to clarify what I mean by education. If education is treated as identical to school attendance, syllabus completion, examination performance, or earning qualifications, then the way I evaluate the system will already be narrowed.

The opposite mistake is also possible: defining education so broadly that every experience becomes education and the concept no longer helps distinguish goals, responsibilities, or outcomes. I need a useful working understanding that is broad enough to reflect education's many purposes, but precise enough to support investigation.

## 2. One word, several uses

The word education can refer to several related things:

- **A process:** activities through which people develop knowledge, understanding, skills, values, or other capacities.
- **An intended outcome:** what a person has learned or become able to understand or do.
- **An institution:** schools, colleges, universities, training providers, and other organizations.
- **A public system:** rules, funding, curriculum, staff, assessments, credentials, and institutions that organize education.
- **A field of thought and practice:** the study and work concerned with how people learn and develop.

These uses are related, but they are not interchangeable. Completing a school programme is an institutional event; what a student actually learns is a different question. A qualification records that specified requirements were met, but it cannot automatically tell us everything about a person's knowledge, judgement, practical ability, or future development.

This distinction is central to my research title. To examine whether a system prepares students for the future or mainly prepares them to pass examinations, I must not confuse activities and credentials with all the outcomes those activities are meant to support.

## 3. What official statistical frameworks contribute

UNESCO's *International Standard Classification of Education (ISCED 2011)* defines an education programme in operational terms: a coherent, organized sequence of educational activities designed to achieve specified learning objectives over time. The objectives can concern knowledge, skills, and competencies in personal, civic, social, or employment contexts. The framework also describes learning as acquisition or modification of knowledge, understanding, attitudes, values, skills, competencies, or behaviours through experience, practice, study, or instruction.

This definition is useful because countries need consistent categories to describe and compare education programmes and qualifications.

However, a statistical classification is designed to support classification and comparison. It does not, by itself, settle what education should be for, whether a particular programme is good, or whether its goals are sufficiently ambitious. A definition useful for comparable data is not automatically a complete theory of education.

**Source:** [UNESCO Institute for Statistics — ISCED 2011](https://uis.unesco.org/sites/default/files/documents/international-standard-classification-of-education-isced-2011-en.pdf).

## 4. What philosophical analysis contributes

The *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* entry on philosophy of education describes long-running debates about the nature and aims of education. One influential strand asks what distinguishes education from mere training or simple growth, and examines education as development of the mind and the ability to understand and participate in valuable forms of thought and practice.

This matters because education is not only a question of whether a person can perform a task. It also raises questions about what is worth learning, why it is worth learning, what kinds of understanding count, and how educational aims should be justified.

Philosophical arguments do not replace empirical research. They help clarify concepts and values, while empirical research can investigate what happens under particular conditions. Both are needed, but they answer different kinds of questions.

**Source:** [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy — Philosophy of Education](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/education-philosophy/).

## 5. Education, learning, and schooling are not synonyms

For this research, I will keep three concepts separate unless a source clearly defines them differently.

### Learning

Learning concerns changes or development in what a person knows, understands, can do, values, or how they respond. It can occur through formal instruction, practice, observation, experience, reading, discussion, and self-directed study. It is possible to attend a lesson without learning what the lesson intended; it is also possible to learn something important outside school.

### Education

Education involves processes and arrangements intended to support learning and development. It may take place in formal institutions, but the broader concept also includes educational activity outside conventional schools. The exact boundary depends on the definition being used, so the source and purpose of each definition must be recorded.

### Schooling

Schooling refers more specifically to organized participation in a school system. It usually includes formal programmes, timetables, teachers, curricula, assessment, and credentials. Schooling is a major way societies organize education, but it should not be treated as identical to every form or purpose of education.

These are provisional working distinctions, not a claim that all researchers use the words in exactly the same way. Step 2 will test and refine them against the literature.

## 6. Instruction, training, and qualifications

**Instruction** is deliberate guidance or communication intended to help someone learn. Receiving instruction does not guarantee learning.

**Training** commonly emphasizes preparation for a task, role, or set of practices. Training can be valuable and can form part of a broader education. The question is not whether training is good or bad, but whether the goals being pursued are sufficient for the context and learner.

**Qualifications** are formal recognitions that requirements have been met. They can help communicate achievement and support progression to further study or employment. But a credential is a representation of achievement under specified rules; it is not a complete description of a person's abilities or potential.

These distinctions will matter later when I examine curriculum, assessment, examinations, technical education, and transitions to work.

## 7. Education as a right and a social responsibility

UNESCO describes education as a fundamental human right and emphasizes access to quality education without discrimination. This introduces a dimension that cannot be reduced to individual performance or economic productivity: whether people have a fair opportunity to participate in education at all.

A rights-based perspective raises questions about access, inclusion, quality, public responsibility, and the treatment of learners. It does not, on its own, tell us which curriculum, teaching method, or assessment system is best. Those decisions require further argument and evidence.

**Source:** [UNESCO — The right to education](https://www.unesco.org/en/right-education).

## 8. Why no single definition should settle the whole research

The sources considered so far serve different purposes:

- an international classification helps make education programmes comparable;
- philosophy examines meaning, value, and the justification of educational aims;
- a rights-based approach clarifies entitlements and responsibilities;
- empirical research can investigate learning and outcomes under particular conditions;
- policy documents describe goals and institutional commitments.

These approaches overlap, but they should not be collapsed into one another. An official definition is not automatically a moral argument; a moral argument is not automatically an empirical finding; and a measured outcome does not automatically represent every purpose of education.

## 9. Provisional working definition

For the purposes of this research, I will provisionally use the following definition:

> **Education is the set of intentional processes and organized arrangements through which people are supported in developing knowledge, understanding, skills, judgement, and other capacities for personal development and participation in life and society. Education may occur in formal institutions and in other settings.**

This is a working definition created for the project, not a quotation from UNESCO or a claim of universal consensus. It is deliberately provisional and must be tested for clarity, breadth, usefulness, and possible blind spots before approval.

## 10. Questions still open

1. Does the working definition give enough attention to culture, values, identity, and intergenerational transmission?
2. How should education distinguish worthwhile development from simply changing a person's behaviour?
3. Which purposes belong specifically to education, and which are responsibilities shared with families, communities, and society?
4. How should the definition treat unplanned learning and experience?
5. How can the research avoid measuring only what is easy to measure?
6. How should competing educational purposes be balanced when they conflict?

These questions will inform Step 3, which examines what education is for, but they should not be settled merely by writing a definition.
