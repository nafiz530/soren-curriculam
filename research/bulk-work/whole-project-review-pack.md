# Whole-Project Bulk Review Pack — Bangladesh Education Research

**Author:** Md. Nafiz  
**Question:** *Is our education system actually preparing students for the future, or is it mainly preparing students to pass examinations?*  
**Status:** Whole-project review draft; NOT approved, NOT merged into `main`, NOT publication-ready.

## 1. Purpose and provisional judgement

The goal is to evaluate Bangladesh's education system against its stated purposes, actual learning, equity, wellbeing, future capability and evidence from other systems, then design a feasible model from early childhood through higher education and leadership. “Perfect education system” is an aspiration, not a claim that one universal model exists. The final proposal must state values, trade-offs, costs, evidence thresholds and how it changes when evidence contradicts it.

Neither “exams are bad” nor “the existing system is good enough” is assumed. Five questions must stay separate: do students learn core knowledge and foundations; can they retain, explain, transfer and use it; do they develop practical, social, civic, digital and self-management capabilities; are opportunities distributed fairly; and do credentials support valid decisions or encourage expensive positional competition?

The evidence located so far does not justify saying Bangladesh's system *only* prepares students for examinations. It does justify investigating whether exam-linked incentives, tutoring, foundational learning gaps, uneven resources and implementation problems cause it to underdeliver on broader purposes. The National Student Assessment 2022 is a primary source for selected learning outcomes; the World Bank Learning Poverty Brief is a standardized foundational-reading indicator. Neither captures every educational purpose.

## 2. Critical evaluation of Bangladesh

### Examination pressure, GPA-5 and tutoring

Possible mechanisms include scarce admissions, grade-based reputation, school/teacher incentives, parental fear that others are gaining an advantage, and test-driven tutoring. Tutoring may provide genuine scaffolding, so it cannot be dismissed as wholly useless. The equity question is whether paid support becomes a necessary competitive advantage.

Alam and Zhu (2022) interviewed 18 students, tutors and parents at two higher-secondary institutions in Sylhet. They describe both perceived score benefits and risks of rote learning, but a small purposive sample and self-report cannot estimate national prevalence or causal effects. Al Amin and Greenwood (2018) report examination pressure and washback among secondary English teachers and stakeholders, but their English focus and sampling limit national claims. These sources justify further research, not a claim that every teacher teaches by rote or every tutoring centre damages curiosity.

Before publication, obtain current representative evidence on tutoring prevalence, household spending, time use, regional/income differences, classroom practice, exam construct coverage and admissions criteria. Separate remedial tutoring from tutoring for positional competition.

### Curriculum and memorization

“ NCTB textbooks are memorization-only” is too broad unless tested subject by subject. Inspect actual textbooks, learning outcomes, teacher guides, exercises, exam papers and classroom practice. Memorization of essential knowledge can support reasoning; the real test is whether students can also explain, apply, evaluate and transfer what they know.

The National Education Policy 2010, National Curriculum 2012 and National Curriculum Framework 2021 are primary sources for stated intent and design—not independent proof of implementation. Reconstruct proposals, approvals, rollouts, revisions and evaluations from dated records. Do not infer political motives from chronology alone.

### Teachers and classroom practice

Avoid describing all Bangladeshi classrooms as lecture-heavy without representative observation data. Measure time spent explaining, questioning, practising, discussing, doing practical work, receiving feedback and waiting; account for class size, materials, language, workload and subject.

The 2026 UNICEF/Government differentiated-teaching pilot in 24 secondary schools suggests that checking current understanding and adapting materials may help, but full design, outcomes, comparison and attrition require appraisal. It is not a national estimate. The implication is to support teachers with materials, coaching and planning time—not blame them for system constraints.

## 3. International comparison: learn features, not national brands

- **Singapore:** Official policy records show changes to assessment load, wider scoring bands, project work and flexible subject pathways while retaining important examinations. This challenges the false choice between academic rigour and reducing unnecessary pressure. Policy pages establish what government did, not whether each measure improved outcomes.
- **Finland:** Its national core curriculum embeds thinking, learning to learn, multiliteracy, ICT, interaction, daily-life management, entrepreneurship and sustainability across subjects. This is a curriculum architecture, not a context-free recipe.
- **Estonia:** OECD PISA 2022 data show performance above the OECD average in mathematics, reading and science, but also a widening socioeconomic mathematics gap from 2018 to 2022. Strong systems still face equity and trend challenges.
- **PISA:** It is a partial comparison for sampled 15-year-olds, not a full ranking of educational value. Bangladesh did not participate in PISA 2022; do not assign it a PISA score or infer causal effects from rankings.

Compare specific features and conditions—teacher preparation, assessment, foundational support, curriculum coherence, vocational routes, digital access and equity—rather than copying a country's brand.

## 4. Principles for a proposed Bangladesh system

1. Strong foundations for everyone: Bangla literacy, additional-language learning, mathematics, science, history/civic knowledge, digital and media literacy.
2. Knowledge plus capability: do not remove content merely to make room for “skills”; use subject knowledge as the base for inquiry and transfer.
3. Developmentally appropriate learning, with more independence and choice as learners mature.
4. Multiple ways to demonstrate learning, with external moderation for consequential decisions.
5. Early support before punishment; no single test should permanently define a child.
6. Teacher professionalism: subject knowledge, coached practice, planning time, autonomy within clear standards and sustained development.
7. Equity by design: disability access, rural/urban support, language-aware teaching, safe schools and offline alternatives.
8. Respected academic, technical and vocational pathways with bridges and route-switching.
9. Bangladesh's languages, history, civic life, environment and economy alongside global science, culture and collaboration.
10. Full recurrent-cost accounting; no unfunded mandates.
11. Independent monitoring, transparent indicators, pilots and stop/revise criteria.
12. Pluralism and safety: evidence-based debate, dignity and cultural/religious diversity without ideological conformity.

## 5. Proposed developmental architecture — for review, not approval

### Early childhood, ages 3–7

**Purpose:** safety, attachment, language, movement, play, curiosity, emotional regulation, social interaction and early number/literacy foundations.

**Experience:** guided free play, stories and conversation, outdoor movement, art/music/drama, counting and pattern games, nature observation and cooperative routines. Short explicit teaching is appropriate when responsive and age-suited. Play must not become an excuse to neglect children who need targeted instruction.

**Assessment:** observation and developmental portfolios; supportive screening to identify support needs, never public rankings or high-stakes exams. Screening is not a diagnosis without professional assessment.

**Safeguards and indicators:** child protection, nutrition/health referral, disability inclusion, family partnership, trained educators and accessible materials. Monitor attendance, safety, language, number sense, social development, curiosity and timely support—not a single readiness score.

### Primary, ages 8–11

**Purpose:** secure reading comprehension, writing, mathematics, science foundations, Bangladeshi/world knowledge, arts, physical education, communication and belonging.

**Teaching:** explicit modelling, guided practice, discussion, retrieval and spaced review, short investigations, reading-rich classrooms and local examples. Group tasks must include individual accountability.

**Assessment:** frequent low-stakes checks; moderated term tasks; brief standardized diagnostics; written tests sampling understanding, not only recall. Report strengths and next steps rather than one rank. Continuous assessment requires clear rubrics, moderation, accommodations and manageable teacher workload.

**Support:** school-day catch-up before families are pushed toward paid tutoring; small-group support based on demonstrated need; homework that does not assume devices, quiet rooms or adult tutors.

### Lower secondary, ages 12–14

**Purpose:** deeper disciplinary knowledge, independence, identity, media literacy, collaboration and exploration.

**Core:** Bangla, English/additional languages, mathematics, sciences, history/geography/civics, arts, physical education, computing/digital literacy, financial basics, age-appropriate health education, climate/environment and practical design.

**Methods:** labs and field investigations, source evaluation, debate, evidence-based writing, local problem projects and making/repair—alongside explicit instruction and guided inquiry. Project work supplements coherent subject teaching.

**Assessment:** low-stakes quizzes, subject tests, oral defence, practical work, writing and portfolios. Use transparent criteria and individual evidence so family wealth or device access does not dominate grades. Students sample academic, technical, artistic and service activities without irreversible early tracking.

### Upper secondary, ages 15–16

**Purpose:** prepare for deeper study, skilled work, citizenship and informed choices without a single terminal examination being the only proof of capability.

**Flexible pathways:** common academic foundations plus concentrations such as sciences, humanities/social sciences, business/entrepreneurship, arts, computing/engineering foundations, agriculture/environment and technical/vocational modules. Students can combine options and later switch with bridging support.

**Assessment:** school coursework and practicals; externally set and moderated subject examinations; diagnostics; capstones/projects where suitable; oral explanation or performance in selected domains. External moderation protects against inconsistent school grading. Consequential decisions should use multiple measures where feasible.

**Certification:** report subject-level proficiency and completed capabilities; publish requirements; offer reassessment. University/scholarship selection should be audited for predictive validity, access and socioeconomic bias.

### Higher secondary and transition, ages 17–20+

Bangladesh's institutional age boundaries do not map perfectly to these age bands. The final model must map to actual grades, HSC, diploma, TVET and tertiary routes without assuming every learner follows one uninterrupted path.

**Routes:** academic/HSC, technical/vocational qualifications, apprenticeships/work-based learning, arts, agriculture/environment, entrepreneurship and bridging/foundation programmes. Every route needs quality standards, recognition and transfer opportunities.

**Experience:** advanced subject study, research/evidence evaluation, internships, supervised workplace projects, communication, ethics, civic service, mentorship, student organizations and career guidance.

**Leadership pipeline:** accessible school/college projects in which students identify a local need, consult affected people, budget, test a solution, report results and reflect on failure. Leadership means service, evidence, teamwork and accountability—not charisma, political conformity or a privilege for top scorers.

**Assessment:** credible subject examinations where required, applied tasks, practical demonstrations, supervised research and internship evaluation with safeguards. Subjective “leadership” ratings must not become hidden admissions filters.

## 6. Assessment architecture: not simply abolish examinations

Use four layers:
1. **Daily formative feedback:** questions, practice, mini-quizzes and actionable feedback; not every task becomes a grade.
2. **Periodic school evidence:** written work, oral explanation, practical demonstrations, projects and portfolios, with transparent rubrics and moderation.
3. **External low-stakes sampling:** independently measure system-level learning and inequity without publicly ranking every child.
4. **Externally moderated certification:** limited, well-designed exams and practical assessments for major qualifications/progression.

Continuous assessment must not mean constant surveillance or constant grading. Ask for every assessment: what construct does it measure; is it reliable enough for this decision; can disabled students access it; how much does it cost; can it be gamed; how much teacher time does it consume; does it increase coaching dependence; and what happens when a student is ill or disadvantaged?

Retain external standards where they protect fairness, while reducing unnecessary high-stakes tests and the dominance of GPA-5 as the definition of success.

## 7. Teacher and school operating model

Pre-service preparation should combine subject knowledge, child development, learning science, inclusive practice, assessment literacy, classroom management and supervised practice. Induction needs mentoring and protected observation/feedback. Professional development should be sustained and practice-linked rather than attendance at isolated workshops.

Schools need common planning time, usable guides, low-cost lab/library/manipulative materials, accessible buildings, safe toilets, connectivity where useful and maintenance. Leadership should observe teaching to support improvement, not only generate punitive paperwork. Recruitment and promotion need transparent criteria. Teacher wellbeing, pay reliability, workload and rural staffing are system variables.

## 8. Equity and student protection

Disaggregate outcomes by income, gender, disability, geography, school type and language where lawful and ethical. A national average can hide exclusion. The MICS 2019 disability-attendance analysis and inclusion studies show why policy language must be checked against access, accommodations and participation.

Minimum entitlement should include safe spaces, accessible materials/assessments, reasonable accommodations, school-based remedial support, safeguarding, anti-bullying procedures, menstrual hygiene, health/nutrition coordination and offline alternatives. Collect only necessary student data, restrict access, set retention rules and prohibit commercial exploitation.

## 9. Technology and AI

Technology is a tool, not a reform by itself. Every proposal must name the problem, best non-digital alternative, total cost, connectivity/language needs, evidence of benefit, teacher support, privacy/security, accessibility and harm-monitoring plan.

AI may assist practice, translation, accessibility, feedback and teacher preparation, but can hallucinate, reproduce bias, expose data, weaken independent effort and perform unevenly in Bangla/Banglish. It should not be the sole authority for grading, diagnosis, discipline or high-stakes placement. Essential learning must have a human/offline route. Apply UNESCO's human-centred guidance and obtain legal review against applicable Bangladesh law.

## 10. Funding and phased implementation

Separate capital and recurring costs: teacher compensation, training/coaching, curriculum/materials, accessibility, maintenance, connectivity, assessment/moderation, student support and independent evaluation. Estimate costs per learner and by geography, enrolment growth and inflation. Do not demand daily projects, portfolios, individualized plans or digital platforms without paying for time, materials, moderation and maintenance.

- **Phase 0 — baseline/co-design:** audit learning, teacher conditions, assessment burden, tutoring expenditure and equity; consult students, parents, teachers, employers, universities and communities.
- **Phase 1 — foundational support pilots:** varied urban/rural and school contexts; test diagnostic teaching, school-day catch-up, balanced assessment and teacher coaching.
- **Phase 2 — independently evaluated expansion:** expand only when predefined learning, equity, workload and cost thresholds are met.
- **Phase 3 — align curriculum and qualifications:** revise textbooks, teacher preparation, assessment and progression together.
- **Phase 4 — public reporting:** publish outcomes, costs, subgroup gaps, implementation failures and corrective action.

Exact budgets, dates, class sizes and ratios require baseline data, costing and stakeholder input.

## 11. Evaluation dashboard

Use a balanced dashboard, not a single composite score:
- foundations: reading, writing, mathematics, scientific reasoning;
- knowledge: subject knowledge and conceptual understanding;
- transfer: novel problems and practical application;
- agency: self-regulation, reflection and help-seeking;
- wellbeing: safety, belonging, workload and support access;
- equity: attendance, completion, accommodations and learning gaps;
- civic/social capability: evidence evaluation, respectful disagreement and collaboration without ideological conformity;
- transition: further study, employment, training, entrepreneurship and route-switching, interpreted in labour-market context;
- teacher conditions: retention, workload, support and classroom-practice measures;
- efficiency: costs, materials, maintenance and value against alternatives.

Use baselines, comparison groups where ethical/feasible, implementation data, mixed methods and pre-specified outcomes. Independent evaluators must report null/adverse findings. Score changes alone cannot prove future leadership or wellbeing.

## 12. Stress tests

| Risk | Warning | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| Continuous assessment becomes constant grading | No respite; every task graded | Keep practice low-stakes, cap formal evidence and audit workload. |
| Projects reward wealth | Project quality tracks household resources | Do core work in school, provide materials, assess individual understanding and moderate. |
| Grade inflation | School marks rise while external samples do not | Moderated samples, exemplars, audits and transparent reporting. |
| Curriculum overload | Coverage drops, teacher stress rises | Remove duplication, protect core knowledge and pilot time budgets. |
| Digital divide | Outcomes track device access | Offline alternatives and no compulsory paid devices. |
| Early tracking entrenches inequality | Disadvantaged students cluster in low-status routes | Delay irreversible selection, allow route switching and audit access. |
| Paperwork displaces teaching | Forms rise without better practice | Remove duplication and fund planning time. |
| Political turnover breaks continuity | Materials/exams change without evaluation | Stable minimum standards, change logs and independent review. |
| Tutoring adapts to new assessments | Coaching sells portfolio/grade advantages | Keep core evidence in school, publish criteria and monitor family costs. |
| Leadership becomes elitist/ideological | Opportunities concentrate among favored students | Universal access, rotating roles, transparent criteria and pluralism. |
| AI weakens independent thinking/privacy | Unverifiable work or sensitive data exposure | Teach AI literacy, protect data, require process evidence and provide human/offline routes. |
| Fewer exams hide learning gaps | No credible independent data | Retain low-stakes national sampling and publish proficiency distributions. |

## 13. Forty-step coverage map

1. Research question: open and bounded; do not assume the verdict.
2. What education is: formal, informal and non-formal learning, institutions and lifelong development.
3. Purpose: knowledge, agency, work, citizenship, wellbeing, culture and plural values.
4. Evidence standards: claim-relative appraisal, traceability, counterarguments, ethics and uncertainty.
5. How people learn: prior knowledge, practice, spacing, retrieval, feedback, context and transfer; no single-method dogma.
6. Development: physical, language, cognitive, social and emotional growth; age bands are not identical for all learners.
7. Motivation: autonomy, competence, belonging, goals, feedback and structure; avoid simplistic motivation claims.
8. Diversity: disability, language, prior attainment, neurodiversity, income and context; avoid fixed ability labels.
9. History: primary-source chronology; distinguish proposal, adoption, rollout and impact.
10. System anatomy: governance, finance, curriculum, books, teachers, assessment, admissions, tutoring, credentials and work.
11. Problems/trade-offs: pressure, learning gaps, inequity, overload, stress and credential competition, with claim-specific limits.
12. Existing strengths: shared knowledge, standards, socialization, credentials and progression; preserve effective functions.
13. Comparison: features and outcomes, not causal league tables.
14. Curriculum: inspect frameworks and actual materials; disciplinary and transversal competence.
15. Assessment: validity, reliability, feedback, fairness, workload, cost, gaming and consequences.
16. Reform: dated decisions, rollouts, reversals and evaluations; no motive inference from chronology.
17. Stages: developmental progression mapped to Bangladesh's real grades/qualifications.
18. Readiness: flexible support and mastery checks; avoid permanent one-test sorting.
19. Knowledge/choice: common foundation and gradually expanding choice.
20. Pathways: equal dignity, quality and bridges across academic/technical/vocational routes.
21. Curriculum principles: coherence, depth, progression, relevance and realistic workload.
22. Core domains: language, mathematics, science, humanities, arts, health, physical, civic, digital and environmental learning.
23. Practical/interdisciplinary: projects rooted in subject knowledge, resources and safeguards.
24. Life capabilities: communication, finance, digital safety, health, self-management, environment and citizenship.
25. Teaching: explicit instruction, guided practice, discussion, retrieval, feedback, inquiry and differentiation according to task.
26. Classroom/time/homework: protect attention, practice, feedback, planning and rest; no blanket class-size claims.
27. Projects: school-based access, individual accountability, transparent rubrics, moderation and cost control.
28. Assessment purpose: diagnose, guide, certify and select require different evidence.
29. Assessment mix: formative, summative and practical evidence with manageable workload and accommodations.
30. Exams/grades: retain justified exams, reduce unnecessary stakes and single-score dominance.
31. Teachers: subject expertise, supervised practice, mentoring, sustained development and leadership support.
32. Technology: compare with non-digital alternatives, total cost, accessibility and maintenance.
33. AI: human-centred, private, age-appropriate, equitable and non-essential when inaccessible.
34. Equity: disaggregate results; inclusion, safety, accommodations and remedial support.
35. Families/governance: transparent accountability, partnership, community voice and continuity without capture.
36. Funding: recurrent costs, phased pilots and no unfunded mandates.
37. Careers: credible pathways, work exposure, career information and flexible transition.
38. Citizenship/lifelong learning: plural civic reasoning, environment, culture and adult learning.
39. Complete proposed system: stage-by-stage architecture, assessment, teachers, equity, technology and finance specified above; proposal for review, not proven optimum.
40. Implementation/evaluation: phases, dashboard, independent evaluation and stop/revise rules specified above.
