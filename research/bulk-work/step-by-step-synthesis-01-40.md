# Step-by-Step Synthesis Supplement — All 40 Steps

**Author:** Md. Nafiz  
**Branch:** `bulk-research-draft`  
**Status:** Complete first-pass synthesis for whole-project review; individual conclusions remain revisable. This is not a systematic review or a publication-ready final manuscript.

This supplement makes the 40-step roadmap reviewable as a connected argument. “Synthesis” means the current working position, its design implication and the evidence gap that could change it. It does not mean every topic has been exhaustively searched.

## Phase 1 — Research foundation

### Step 1 — Research question, objectives and scope
The core question is whether Bangladesh's education system prepares learners for future life or primarily for examinations. It is a question to investigate, not a pre-decided verdict. “Future preparation” should be operationalized through foundational knowledge, retention and transfer, practical competence, wellbeing, agency, citizenship, equitable access and transitions to further study/work. Grades and examination results remain relevant but are not sufficient measures. The analysis must distinguish the national system's stated aims, classroom experience, measured outcomes and causal explanations. The final review should report what is known, unknown and disputed rather than force a binary answer.

### Step 2 — What is education?
Education includes formal schooling, non-formal provision, family/community learning, vocational training, informal learning and lifelong development. Schooling is a major public institution but not the entirety of education. A narrow definition risks treating certification as learning; an overly broad definition risks making evaluation impossible. The study therefore evaluates school-system responsibilities while recognizing family, health, labour markets, social protection, language and community contexts that influence outcomes. The final proposal must specify what schools can reasonably deliver, what requires inter-agency support and what is a wider societal responsibility.

### Step 3 — What is education for?
Legitimate aims include knowledge, intellectual development, livelihood, agency, wellbeing, citizenship, cultural continuity, social cooperation, ethical reflection, creativity and lifelong learning. These aims can conflict over time, resources and assessment. A future-ready system cannot maximize every outcome simultaneously; it must state priorities and trade-offs transparently. Bangladesh's National Education Policy 2010 and curriculum documents should be mapped against these aims using exact wording. Policy statements show intended purpose, not whether it was achieved. Moral, cultural and spiritual aims require respectful pluralistic discussion and must not be used to suppress evidence-based inquiry or students' dignity.

### Step 4 — Methodology and evidence standards
The approved Step 4 protocol governs this project: claim-relative appraisal, source traceability, construct definition, explicit separation of evidence/inference/value judgement, counterarguments, bias/funder checks, Bangla/English translation care, ethics and an appropriate systematic-review trigger. Primary policy sources establish what was written or decided; national datasets establish measured patterns within their design; qualitative work illuminates experience and mechanisms; experiments can estimate causal effects only for the tested population/intervention; international meta-analyses support general hypotheses but require transfer analysis. Search logs must disclose incomplete coverage. A source count is not a quality score. The project must not call itself a systematic review unless inclusion criteria, reproducible searches, screening and appraisal have actually been completed.

## Phase 2 — Understanding the learner

### Step 5 — How humans learn
Learning is multidimensional: immediate performance, durable retention, conceptual understanding, transfer, procedural fluency and independent self-regulation are related but distinct. Prior knowledge, attention, working-memory limits, practice, spacing, retrieval, feedback, explanation and metacognition can matter, but effects depend on learner, subject, goal and implementation. Dunlosky et al. (2013) rated practice testing and distributed practice highly useful in their review; later reviews support spacing/retrieval while noting contextual variability. A 2025 classroom distributed-practice meta-analysis reported a positive average effect but high heterogeneity and limited moderator analysis. Active-learning meta-analyses in undergraduate STEM must not be numerically generalized to Bangladeshi school students. No single method should replace instruction universally.

### Step 6 — Child and adolescent development
Development includes language, cognition, executive function, physical coordination, identity, emotional regulation, sleep, peer relationships and growing autonomy. These dimensions interact; age bands are useful for planning but do not define each child's readiness. Early-years education should provide play, conversation, movement and secure relationships alongside responsive targeted instruction. Primary years need systematic foundations and feedback; adolescence increasingly supports abstraction, identity, independence and meaningful choice. The model should not force early high-stakes ranking or treat developmental screening as diagnosis. For Bangladesh, the remaining evidence task is to source local child-development, health, nutrition, disability and language data and assess how family responsibilities, school calendars and resource differences affect readiness.

### Step 7 — Motivation, engagement and independence
Motivation is influenced by interest, perceived value, confidence, belonging, autonomy, goals, habits, feedback and external incentives. Neither “intrinsic motivation always works” nor “rewards always work” is defensible. High-stakes marks can motivate effort but can also shift attention toward short-term score optimization, avoidance of difficult tasks and comparison with peers. Student agency requires structured choice and clear expectations, not abandonment of teaching. The proposed system should give students meaningful choices over projects and pathways while maintaining core knowledge and support. Measure engagement and persistence alongside learning, and investigate differences across income, gender, disability and school context rather than treating disengagement as a personal defect.

### Step 8 — Individual differences and student diversity
Learners differ in prior knowledge, pace, language, disability, health, interests, family obligations, confidence and access to learning resources. Equity does not mean identical support for everyone, nor does it mean sorting children into fixed ability categories. Use formative evidence to adapt instruction, accessible materials and accommodations; preserve high expectations while giving different routes to mastery. Disability-related attendance analysis using Bangladesh MICS 2019 provides a national-data starting point but is cross-sectional and dated. Qualitative inclusion studies can reveal barriers but may not represent the country. The final design needs current disaggregated data, universal design principles, specialist support, language-aware teaching and safeguards against early tracking that reproduces socioeconomic disadvantage.

## Phase 3 — Understanding existing education

### Step 9 — History of formal education
Modern schooling combines religious and community traditions, colonial administration, nation-building, industrial-era standardization, bureaucratic credentialing and contemporary mass education. Bangladesh's history must be reconstructed from dated primary sources: policy proposals, enactments, curriculum approvals, rollout circulars, examinations, budget decisions and evaluation reports. The National Education Policy 2010, Curriculum 2012 and Curriculum Framework 2021 should be placed on a verified timeline with their formal status and implementation reach. A new policy's publication does not prove nationwide adoption; rollout does not prove fidelity or impact. Political turnover may be relevant to continuity, but motive must not be inferred from chronology alone.

### Step 10 — Anatomy of the modern system
Outcomes arise from interacting institutions: ministries, curriculum authorities, boards, school management, teachers, students, families, tutoring providers, publishers, universities, employers and technology providers. Incentives matter: an examination can shape teaching even when the written curriculum has broader aims. Funding and staffing determine whether policy is feasible; admissions rules determine which signals families value; textbook and teacher-guide quality affect classroom practice. Map public, private, madrasa, technical/vocational and non-formal routes, including transitions and dropout points. The current evidence map is not yet a complete institutional audit; primary legal mandates, financing flows and comparable data by stream must be verified before the final system design.

### Step 11 — Problems, trade-offs and unintended consequences
Hypotheses include weak foundational learning, exam washback, unequal tutoring, overloaded curricula, inconsistent feedback, exclusion, stress, credential competition and uneven school resources. These must be investigated separately rather than bundled into one claim that “the system is broken.” The same mechanism can have benefits and harms: exams can provide common standards while narrowing instruction; tutoring can remedy gaps while intensifying positional competition; projects can develop application while increasing cost and subjectivity. Each problem statement should name the affected group, scale, outcome measure, plausible cause and counterevidence. The current draft supports a structured investigation, not a definitive national causal ranking of these problems.

### Step 12 — What existing systems get right
Existing systems provide shared knowledge, structured progression, literacy/numeracy instruction, trained adults, peer interaction, routine, safeguarding, recognized qualifications and public accountability. Standardization can protect fairness when school resources and teacher grading vary. Subject expertise and cumulative curricula matter; a reform that removes too much content may weaken the knowledge needed for reasoning. Examinations can identify attainment and support selection when validity and fairness are adequate. The proposal should preserve these functions while reducing avoidable harms. A fair evaluation asks which elements work, for whom, at what cost and under what conditions—not only what needs replacing.

## Phase 4 — Comparative and international evidence

### Step 13 — Comparative education systems
Compare systems by a specific question, not by prestige. Singapore illustrates deliberate assessment-load changes alongside strong academic standards; Finland illustrates transversal competences embedded across subjects; Estonia offers evidence on performance and equity in PISA. These are distinct contexts, not one unified “progressive model.” OECD PISA 2022 data show Estonia above the OECD average in assessed domains, while some trends and socioeconomic gaps still require attention. Bangladesh did not participate in PISA 2022. Comparisons must examine uncertainty, demographics, resources, teacher preparation, language, governance and other outcomes. Country rankings do not identify causal policies and should not be used as proof that copying a policy will work.

### Step 14 — Curriculum and school models
Subject-based curricula can build coherent disciplinary knowledge; competency frameworks can make application and broader aims explicit. Either can fail: content can become overloaded and recall-driven, while vague competencies can become hard to teach or assess. Finland's framework demonstrates one way to embed transversal aims in subject learning. Bangladesh's NCTB materials must be analyzed directly: learning outcomes, textbook explanations, tasks, teacher guides, examination alignment and classroom implementation. Do not equate a curriculum's official rhetoric with classroom reality. The design implication is a coherent, cumulative core plus meaningful cross-disciplinary applications, with explicit teacher support and workload testing.

### Step 15 — Assessment and examination models
Assessment may diagnose needs, guide instruction, certify attainment, select applicants, monitor systems or provide accountability; those purposes require different designs. Standardized tests can be scalable and comparable but sample limited constructs. Coursework, oral explanation, practical work and portfolios can reveal different capabilities but may increase cost, subjectivity, copying and unequal home support. A 2024 K–12 meta-analysis of formative assessment reported a positive average effect, but this does not prove that every continuous-assessment policy works. Formative assessment means information changes what happens next, not simply more marks. The proposed model combines low-stakes feedback, moderated school evidence, external sampling and limited externally moderated certification.

### Step 16 — Educational reform: lessons and failures
A policy may fail because it is poorly designed, underfunded, misunderstood, misaligned with examinations, introduced too quickly or unsupported by materials and teacher development. A reform may also be sound in principle but have weak evidence of impact. Build a chronology distinguishing proposal, approval, rollout, revision and evaluation. For Bangladesh, verify curriculum-reform timelines against official circulars and independent analysis; do not infer intent from party chronology. Use pilots, implementation data, teacher workload measures and independent evaluation before scale-up. Stable standards and transparent revision processes can protect continuity without freezing ineffective practices. The remaining evidence need is a systematic reform-by-reform case record with outcomes and costs.

## Phase 5 — Defining the learner's journey

### Step 17 — Educational stages and progression
A developmentally coherent system needs early childhood, primary, lower secondary, upper secondary, technical/vocational routes, tertiary transition and lifelong learning. Stage definitions should reflect learning progression, not only administrative convenience. The proposed ages—3–7, 8–11, 12–14, 15–16 and 17–20+—are planning bands, not a claim that all Bangladesh institutions align perfectly. The final architecture must map to existing grades, HSC, diploma, madrasa, TVET and tertiary routes, including children who enter late, repeat, work, move or return to education. Each stage needs a minimum entitlement, progression criteria, support for gaps and a credible route forward.

### Step 18 — Age, readiness and progression
Age-based cohorts simplify staffing and peer relationships; mastery checks can identify learners advancing with serious gaps. Strict mastery progression, however, can create bottlenecks, stigma and cost if support is unavailable. Readiness should be assessed for specific skills and used to adapt instruction, not label a child's overall potential. Use flexible grouping, short diagnostic cycles, catch-up support and reassessment. Avoid high-stakes sorting based on a single test, especially when language, disability, illness or unequal prior opportunity affects performance. The system needs transparent promotion criteria and data on grade repetition, over-age learners, dropout and learning gains before choosing the balance between age progression and mastery-based supports.

### Step 19 — Core knowledge versus choice
Every learner needs a shared foundation in languages, mathematics, science, history/social understanding, civic reasoning, health, digital/media literacy and practical competence. Choice is valuable for motivation and specialization but can become unequal if only affluent schools offer rich options or if students are tracked too early. The proposed design therefore guarantees a common core, then gradually expands electives and concentrations with guidance and bridges. Subject allocation must be evidence-informed and realistic for teacher supply. A final timetable should be tested for total learning time, depth, cultural/language accessibility, teacher workload and opportunity costs rather than assuming every proposed skill needs a separate subject.

### Step 20 — Academic, technical and vocational pathways
Academic and vocational routes should offer different forms of depth without encoding a hierarchy of human worth. Technical pathways require qualified instructors, equipment, safety, employer partnerships, recognized credentials and clear links to further learning. They must not become a default destination for disadvantaged students. Students should be able to combine modules, change routes and bridge into higher education when they meet transparent prerequisites. For Bangladesh, verify TVET enrolment, completion, employment outcomes, wage returns, employer demand and gender/disability access. A “leadership pipeline” must include technical, community, entrepreneurial and public-service leadership—not only university admission.

## Phase 6 — Curriculum architecture

### Step 21 — Curriculum design principles
The curriculum should be coherent, cumulative, age-appropriate, manageable, culturally and linguistically accessible, evidence-informed and aligned with assessment and teacher capacity. Distinguish broad aims, subject content, practice opportunities and assessment evidence. Protect depth in core knowledge; avoid adding every desirable capability as another standalone subject. Teach transversal skills through meaningful subject tasks while ensuring explicit instruction when a capability is new. Every proposed addition must identify what time or content it replaces, teacher training required, materials and how success is measured. The final curriculum requires a workload audit and pilot before national rollout.

### Step 22 — Core subject domains
Languages build communication and access to knowledge; mathematics and science support quantitative and empirical reasoning; humanities and social sciences provide historical, geographic, civic and cultural understanding; arts develop expression and interpretation; physical education and health support physical capability and wellbeing; computing and media literacy help learners navigate digital systems; environmental learning addresses local and global sustainability. Financial literacy, communication and practical skills should be integrated without displacing core knowledge. Exact allocations require a time-budget study, subject-specific evidence and stakeholder input. Ensure Bangla is supported as a learning language while students gain access to additional languages without treating home-language differences as deficits.

### Step 23 — Practical, interdisciplinary and real-world learning
Projects, laboratories, fieldwork, demonstrations and real-world problems can help learners apply knowledge, but only when goals are clear, background knowledge is adequate, guidance is available and individual learning can be assessed. A project should not be a decorative poster or a costly household production. Keep essential work within school; provide materials; structure milestones; require each student to explain their reasoning; and use transparent rubrics. Interdisciplinary work should connect identifiable subject concepts rather than blur all disciplines. Bangladesh projects could investigate water, agriculture, transport, public health, energy, local history or small-business budgeting, with safety and local feasibility. Compare learning and cost against well-designed conventional instruction.

### Step 24 — Life skills and future capabilities
Communication, collaboration, financial basics, digital safety, media evaluation, problem-solving, decision-making, self-management, health literacy, career exploration, environmental awareness and civic participation are plausible future capabilities. Their labels are not enough: each needs observable learning outcomes and age-appropriate practice. Avoid assuming exact future jobs can be predicted. Integrate capabilities into subjects and school life—for example, budgeting in mathematics, source evaluation in history/science, teamwork in a supervised project, and digital safety in computing. Mental-health education must be supportive and not substitute for clinical services. Evaluate skill retention and application, not just self-reported confidence.

## Phase 7 — Teaching and learning environment

### Step 25 — Teaching methodology
No method is universally superior across ages, subjects and goals. Explicit instruction, worked examples and guided practice can be important for new or complex material; inquiry, discussion, retrieval, explanation and projects can deepen application when properly supported. “Student-led” should not mean students are left without guidance, and “teacher-led” should not mean passive copying all lesson. Choose methods according to the intended learning outcome and prior knowledge. Teacher development should focus on practical routines: checking understanding, feedback, modelling reasoning, structured discussion and differentiation. Observe actual classroom practice and student learning rather than judging pedagogy by labels.

### Step 26 — Classroom, timetable, homework and environment
Time, class size, noise, safety, materials, schedule, sleep, homework and access to quiet study influence learning, but isolated changes may not solve deeper issues. A Campbell review of class-size evidence included many studies but only a smaller subset in meta-analysis; this cautions against blanket claims that class size never matters or that reducing it alone fixes learning. Homework should have a clear purpose, appropriate quantity, feedback and no assumption of private tutoring, devices or parental expertise. Timetables should protect practice, physical activity, arts, teacher planning and rest. Bangladesh-specific classroom observation and cost evidence are required before setting universal class-size or homework limits.

### Step 27 — Practical and project-based learning
Projects are defensible when linked to learning goals, subject knowledge, structured milestones, feedback and individual accountability. They are vulnerable to unequal family help, plagiarism, subjective grading and resource demands. A fair design makes the core project in school, offers accessible materials, uses common rubrics and asks each learner to explain the process and result. Projects should not replace explicit teaching or be used in every subject by default. Evaluate whether projects add transfer, motivation or retention beyond a credible alternative, and report cost and teacher workload. The proposed system includes projects as one method in a balanced curriculum, not as the universal teaching model.

## Phase 8 — Assessment and examinations

### Step 28 — Purpose and design of assessment
Assessment can diagnose, guide, certify, select, monitor or hold institutions accountable. Validity concerns whether the evidence supports the intended interpretation and use; reliability concerns consistency; fairness includes access and differential consequences. A high-stakes exam can be well-designed for some constructs yet miss practical, oral or collaborative competence. A portfolio can add evidence but may be less comparable. Before adopting any assessment, state the decision it supports, target construct, scoring method, accommodations, costs, error consequences and how it can be gamed. Do not turn every learning activity into a grade.

### Step 29 — Formative, summative and practical assessment
Formative assessment is useful when evidence changes the next instructional move. Summative assessment records attainment at a point in time. Practical, oral and portfolio evidence can assess capabilities written tests miss, but require rubrics, moderation, accessible tasks and workload limits. The 2024 meta-analysis of 118 K–12 studies found a positive average effect for formative assessment, while a separate reading-focused meta-analysis reported a smaller positive average and cautioned that cultural context matters. These results support trying high-quality feedback, not assuming continuous assessment automatically improves learning. The system should use low-stakes checks, periodic moderated work, external sampling and selected external certification.

### Step 30 — Examinations, grades and high-stakes decisions
Exams can measure selected knowledge and skills, provide a common signal and support scalable selection. They can also narrow curriculum, reward coaching, encourage strategic omission and turn marks into a proxy for a student's worth. GPA-5 pressure should be investigated as a social and institutional mechanism, not treated as a proven single cause of every educational problem. Retain exams where common external standards are valuable; reduce unnecessary high-stakes events and over-fine ranking; use multiple measures for consequential decisions where feasible. Publish score meaning and limitations, provide reasonable accommodations and reassessment, and monitor whether assessment reforms shift coaching markets rather than reducing pressure.

## Phase 9 — Teachers, technology and AI

### Step 31 — Teacher role and professional development
Teachers explain, diagnose, model disciplinary reasoning, organize practice, give feedback, create safe environments, adapt instruction and assess progress. Accountability without training, time, materials and manageable workload is unlikely to improve practice. Professional learning should combine subject expertise, supervised practice, mentoring, observation, feedback and sustained follow-up. The 2026 differentiated-teaching pilot suggests the value of ongoing support, but full technical appraisal and replication are needed. Bangladesh evidence should cover teacher preparation, staffing, absenteeism, workload, deployment, rural vacancies and classroom practice. The final system must cost professional development and remove low-value administrative work.

### Step 32 — Technology in education
Technology can widen access to resources, support accessibility, feedback, simulation and administration. It can also distract, create maintenance costs, expose data, amplify inequality or digitize weak pedagogy. UNESCO's technology-in-education work emphasizes that appropriateness and evidence matter more than novelty. Each tool should be compared with a non-digital alternative, and judged on learning gain, accessibility, Bangla support, connectivity, total cost, maintenance, teacher training and privacy. Avoid making devices or paid subscriptions a prerequisite for basic education. Bangladesh's infrastructure and household access vary; pilots need offline alternatives and disaggregated evaluation.

### Step 33 — Artificial intelligence and future learning
AI may support practice, explanation, translation, accessibility, formative feedback and teacher preparation. Risks include confident errors, fabricated sources, privacy violations, bias, overdependence, cheating and unequal Bangla/Banglish performance. UNESCO's human-centred guidance calls for privacy protection, age-appropriate use, ethical validation and coherent policy. AI should not be the sole authority for grading, diagnosis, discipline or high-stakes placement. Students need to learn when to use AI, verify output, disclose assistance and work independently. Every essential learning service needs a human/offline alternative. The final proposal requires local legal review, student-data governance and evaluation against the best available non-AI option.

## Phase 10 — Equity, governance and resources

### Step 34 — Equity, accessibility and student protection
Equity includes access to qualified teachers, instructional time, materials, language support, safety, disability accommodations and fair assessment. National averages can hide large gaps. Bangladesh MICS 2019 analysis of children with disabilities is a useful national-data source, but cross-sectional data do not establish causality and may not represent current conditions. Include safeguarding, anti-bullying, accessible buildings/materials, menstrual hygiene, health referrals, reasonable accommodations and remediation. Disaggregate outcomes only where ethical and lawful; protect privacy. Design the minimum entitlement so that rural, low-income, disabled and digitally disconnected learners can participate without paying for private support.

### Step 35 — Families, leadership and governance
Families affect attendance, expectations, tutoring, language exposure and study conditions, but cannot replace a functioning school. School leaders should support teaching quality, safe environments, teacher learning and transparent communication. Governance should clarify responsibilities across ministries, boards, school management and local authorities; publish understandable data; and provide channels for students, parents and teachers to report problems. Stable minimum standards can protect continuity across political change, while evidence-based review allows improvement. Do not attribute motives without direct evidence. The model needs conflict-of-interest rules for tutoring, procurement, assessment and student data, and meaningful participation from rural communities and underrepresented groups.

### Step 36 — Resources, funding and low-resource implementation
Every proposal has capital and recurring costs: salaries, training, materials, buildings, transport, connectivity, devices, assessment, maintenance, monitoring and opportunity costs. A model that works only in affluent urban schools is not a national design. Cost each component per learner and by geography; test teacher supply and implementation time; fund recurring maintenance; and provide low-cost alternatives. Education-budget allocations are not the same as actual spending, per-student resources or effectiveness. Verify original/revised/actual figures and denominators before comparison. Begin with pilots, independent evaluation and explicit expansion criteria. Do not mandate projects, portfolios or digital systems without paying for teacher time and materials.

## Phase 11 — Education beyond school

### Step 37 — Higher education, careers and workforce transition
School should build strong foundations and help learners understand options, but cannot predict exact future jobs. Labour markets change and employer demand is not the only purpose of education. The proposal needs credible academic, technical, vocational, apprenticeship, entrepreneurship and community-service routes, with career guidance and opportunities to switch. Evaluate transition using employment quality, further study, training, wages where appropriate, job satisfaction and underemployment—not employment rate alone. Bangladesh evidence should include graduate tracer studies, TVET outcomes, credential returns and skill mismatch. Avoid turning schooling into narrow job training or assuming a degree automatically proves competence.

### Step 38 — Citizenship, society and lifelong learning
Education can support informed civic participation, source evaluation, respectful disagreement, cultural understanding, social responsibility, environmental awareness and continued learning. These aims need actual practice: evidence-based discussion, community projects, history and civic knowledge, media literacy and reflection on public problems. Assessment should reward reasoning and respectful engagement, not agreement with a political or religious viewpoint. Cultural and religious literacy can be taught alongside pluralism and protection of conscience. Lifelong learning requires accessible adult education, digital skills and routes back into formal qualifications. The final framework should define these outcomes in observable but non-indoctrinating terms and include feedback from diverse Bangladeshi communities.

## Phase 12 — Design, implementation and defence

### Step 39 — Complete proposed education system
The proposed model in the companion [whole-project review pack](whole-project-review-pack.md) covers ages 3–7, 8–11, 12–14, 15–16 and 17–20+, and includes core knowledge, flexible pathways, teacher support, practical learning, balanced assessment, inclusion, technology/AI governance, funding and leadership opportunities. It is deliberately a proposal for review, not a proven optimum. It should be tested against the current system and plausible alternatives on learning, equity, cost, workload, wellbeing and transition outcomes. The model must preserve the benefits of shared knowledge and credible qualifications while reducing unnecessary exam pressure. Author review should identify value choices and missing Bangladesh-specific requirements before any final recommendation is locked.

### Step 40 — Implementation, evaluation, stress testing and final framework
The proposed sequence is baseline/co-design, small pilots, independent evaluation, phased expansion and public reporting. The evaluation dashboard covers foundations, knowledge, transfer, agency, wellbeing, equity, civic/social capability, transitions, teacher conditions and cost. Stress tests include continuous-assessment overload, project inequity, grade inflation, curriculum overload, digital exclusion, early tracking, paperwork, political discontinuity, tutoring-market adaptation, elitist leadership programmes and AI privacy risks. Predefine outcomes and stop/revise thresholds before pilots; include null and adverse findings. Do not announce national rollout before costing, teacher preparation and baseline measurement. The final framework should include a theory of change, implementation responsibilities, risk register, budget model, data governance, independent review and a public revision process.

## Cross-step synthesis: the key design choice

The evidence points away from a binary choice between “exams” and “no exams.” The central design problem is aligning curriculum, teaching, assessment, admissions, teacher support, resources and incentives so that students learn important knowledge and can use it. A balanced model should retain credible external evidence for certification while reducing unnecessary high-stakes ranking, make foundational support available inside school, build practical and civic capability without sacrificing disciplinary depth, and evaluate equity and costs as rigorously as average scores.

## Evidence boundary

This first-pass synthesis draws on primary policy sources, official curriculum/assessment sources, peer-reviewed studies, international reviews and policy guidance identified in the linked source register. Search coverage—especially Bangla-language databases, local primary records and representative current data—is incomplete. Exact National Student Assessment values, policy chronology, budget figures, tutoring prevalence, classroom observations, reform impacts and costing remain priority verification tasks. This file is ready for the author's whole-project review as a research draft; it is not represented as exhaustive or publication-ready.
