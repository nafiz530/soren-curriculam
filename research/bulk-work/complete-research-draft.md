## How to read this dossier

This is a structured, evidence-qualified synthesis, not a claim that every question has already been answered exhaustively. “Preliminary finding” means the evidence located so far supports a working interpretation; it does not mean the approved Step 4 threshold for “established evidence” has been met. Search coverage remains uneven, especially for Bangla-language research, local primary records, longitudinal evidence, and direct comparisons within Bangladesh. Those gaps are recorded rather than concealed.

The central question is deliberately open. Neither “exams are bad” nor “the existing system is good enough” is assumed. The research distinguishes learning, certification, selection, signaling, socialization, equity, well-being, citizenship, practical competence, and future adaptability.

## Executive synthesis — provisional

The evidence reviewed so far supports a cautious conclusion: **an education system can expand access, teach useful knowledge, certify attainment, and still fail to ensure that every learner develops foundational literacy, numeracy, durable understanding, transfer, practical capability, and agency.** Bangladesh-specific sources make the gap between access and learning a serious question. A UNICEF Innocenti report drawing on the 2022 National Student Assessment reports that about half of Grade 5 children could not read at grade level and more than two-thirds could not perform basic counting after primary education; it also reports Bangla proficiency improving by six percentage points between 2017 and 2022 while mathematics proficiency declined by two points. These are secondary-report figures that must be traced to the original NSA/DPE tables before they become load-bearing statistics. See the [UNICEF Innocenti report](https://www.unicef.org/innocenti/innocenti/innocenti/media/9341/file/UNICEF-Innocenti-Child-labour-schooling-report-2024.pdf) and the [official National Student Assessment portal](https://nsa.ipemis.dpe.gov.bd/?lang=en_EN).

The evidence does **not** support the simplistic conclusion that examinations should disappear or that a single fashionable teaching style can replace teaching. Examinations can provide common information, certification, feedback, and selection; their validity depends on what they measure, the stakes attached to them, the fairness of access to preparation, and the consequences they create. Teaching, practice, memory, explanation, feedback, projects, and assessment are not mutually exclusive. The strongest design question is how to align curriculum, instruction, assessment, teacher support, and institutional incentives with clearly defined educational purposes.

A recent Bangladesh classroom pilot is especially relevant: the 2026 UNICEF Innocenti working paper *Drivers of Learning in Differentiated Classrooms* reports two years of evidence from 24 secondary schools. Checking students' understanding at their current level was the practice most consistently associated with better learning; varied learning materials helped particularly in mathematics; group work helped in Bangla but not mathematics when individual accountability was weak; very large classes reduced effectiveness; and teacher confidence in checking progress declined over the year. These are promising context-specific findings, but the paper is a working paper about a particular intervention and should not be treated as a national causal estimate or a universal recipe. [Source](https://www.unicef.org/innocenti/reports/drivers-learning-differentiated-classrooms).

Bangladesh's official policy record also complicates any claim that policy documents simply endorse rote examination preparation. The 2010 National Education Policy includes objectives concerning curiosity and thoughtfulness rather than rote learning, practical/vocational skills, joyful learning environments, moral development, climate awareness, quality and continuity across stages, and ICT. The 2012 National Curriculum describes an objective–learning-outcome model, with cognitive, affective and psychomotor domains. These stated intentions are not proof of implementation or impact. Policy intent, curriculum content, teacher practice, assessment incentives, resources, and student outcomes must be examined separately. [National Education Policy 2010 (official-hosted PDF)](https://dpe.portal.gov.bd/sites/default/files/files/dpe.portal.gov.bd/files/96a9aad5_16e6_414f_91e9_ba07a4746464/Education%20Policy%202010.pdf); [National Curriculum 2012 (NCTB PDF)](https://nctb.gov.bd/sites/default/files/files/nctb.portal.gov.bd/files/6d9b9671_f815_460c_b8ef_c58a1b829f55/English.pdf).

International comparisons can identify possible mechanisms and design choices, but cannot be copied mechanically. PISA 2022 provides comparable performance data for participating systems, not a complete ranking of educational worth. Singapore and Japan perform strongly on PISA literacy/numeracy/science measures, but comparisons must also account for selection, curriculum, teacher capacity, tutoring, social context, equity, student well-being, and what PISA does not measure. Bangladesh is not a PISA 2022 participant in the country notes cited here, so these results cannot be presented as a direct Bangladesh score comparison. [OECD PISA 2022 results](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/pisa-2022-results-volume-i_53f23881-en.html); [Singapore country note](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/pisa-2022-results-volume-i-and-ii-country-notes_ed6fbcc5-en/singapore_2f72624e-en.html); [Japan country note](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/pisa-2022-results-volume-i-and-ii-country-notes_ed6fbcc5-en/japan_f7d7daad-en.html).

The defensible provisional direction is therefore **alignment, not a slogan**: define outcomes; secure foundational learning; preserve essential disciplinary knowledge; teach for both fluency and understanding; use assessment for diagnosis as well as certification; support teachers and schools; reduce unjust barriers; and test proposed changes against costs, unintended consequences, local conditions, and credible counterarguments. This is a working interpretation, not a final system recommendation.

# Phase 1 — Research Foundation

## Step 1. Research Question, Objectives & Scope — previously approved

The central question asks whether the education system prepares learners for future life or primarily for examinations. “Preparing for the future” must be operationalized rather than left as a slogan. Candidate dimensions include foundational knowledge, disciplinary understanding, transfer, practical skill, communication, reasoning, self-regulation, well-being, citizenship, ethical judgment, work transitions, and lifelong learning. Examination preparation is not itself evidence of failure: examinations may be one useful outcome and tool, but become a distortion when the measured construct is narrower than the intended educational purpose.

The study is document-based at present. It should not imply original interviews, surveys, classroom observations, or nationally representative causal estimates that have not been conducted. The target is a defensible evidence synthesis and proposed framework, with explicit limitations and a later implementation/evaluation plan.

## Step 2. What Is Education? — previously approved

Education is simultaneously a process of learning, a public institution, a family and community practice, a route to credentials, a means of social participation, and a contested allocation of opportunities. A narrow definition as “schooling” misses informal learning and adult learning; a definition as “whatever develops a person” can become too broad to evaluate. The project should distinguish formal schooling, non-formal education, informal learning, instruction, assessment, certification, and education as a social institution.

No one disciplinary lens is sufficient. Cognitive science helps explain learning mechanisms; philosophy clarifies aims and values; sociology examines reproduction, credentials, and power; economics examines incentives and opportunity costs; history explains inherited structures; comparative education studies contextual alternatives; and policy analysis distinguishes formal commitments from implementation.

## Step 3. What Is Education For? — previously approved, open evidence obligations remain

Educational aims can include knowledge, personal development, livelihood, citizenship, cultural continuity, social cohesion, equality of opportunity, well-being, moral or spiritual development, creativity, and continued learning. These aims can conflict. A curriculum has finite time; expanding one requirement may displace another. The appropriate balance is a normative decision informed—but not settled—by evidence.

The official Bangladesh National Education Policy 2010 contains objectives that include resisting rote learning, developing curiosity and thoughtfulness, practical and vocational skill, moral character, a joyful learning environment, climate awareness, ICT, and continuity of knowledge and skills across stages. The National Curriculum 2012 also articulates an objective-to-learning-outcome model. The next phase must compare these intentions against later curricula, assessment patterns, implementation reports, and outcomes. It must not infer successful implementation from policy language.

Carry-forward tasks: verify exact policy clauses; trace 2012 revision and grade-by-grade rollout from primary records; distinguish proposals, commissions, formal adoption, and implementation; examine coaching and examination washback; study climate education, pluralism, moral/spiritual development, credential competition, and political continuity without attributing motives from chronology alone.

## Step 4. Research Methodology & Evidence Standards — approved working method

The approved protocol remains binding. Claims must be appraised relative to their type, population, setting, measures, and intended use. Load-bearing statistics must be traced to original tables or datasets; multiple publications repeating one dataset do not constitute independent evidence. Search logs should record queries, language, date, scope, and limitations. Each substantial source needs an SRC record and source-register entry. High-impact conclusions require a devil's-advocate pass and explicit uncertainty. Normative premises and empirical findings must be kept separate.

This dossier is not a substitute for completing the protocol artifacts. Each step still needs source-level provenance, claim-level evidence tables, and a recorded adversarial review before any high-impact recommendation can be considered settled.

### Step 4 application: credential competition and examination pressure — draft, unapproved

The plausible mechanism is not simply that students or teachers “like exams.” Credentials can allocate scarce educational and occupational opportunities; when selection relies on a limited number of signals, families and institutions may rationally invest in test preparation. This can create washback: content and teaching time shift toward what is tested, while less tested capabilities receive less attention. Spence's signaling model, Dore's *The Diploma Disease*, Collins's credentialism, and Hirsch's positional competition offer different explanatory lenses. They are theories, not direct proof of Bangladesh's current causal mechanisms.

The Bangladesh examination-system study by Al Amin and Greenwood (2018) reported teacher, student, parent and institutional pressures related to grades, coaching, reputation, and test-focused content. Its design and context limit national generalization. The next pass must connect these mechanisms to current admissions/hiring rules, coaching data, exam specifications, and Bangladesh-specific quantitative or qualitative studies. The 2014–2015 tutoring estimates found in the literature must be labelled historical and subject-specific, not current all-subject national prevalence. The ADB tablet-plus-tutoring intervention for out-of-school children must not be misused as proof that commercial tutoring for exam preparation improves mainstream secondary education.

# Phase 2 — Understanding the Learner

## Step 5. How Humans Learn — draft, not approved

Learning is multidimensional: immediate performance, durable retention, conceptual understanding, transfer, procedural fluency, and independent self-regulation are related but not interchangeable outcomes. A student may perform well after rehearsal without retaining knowledge; may remember facts without applying them; or may struggle on a timed test while possessing understanding not well captured by that format.

The learning-science literature supports attention to prior knowledge, memory limits, practice, retrieval, spacing, feedback, explanation, metacognition, context, language, and disciplinary knowledge. Dunlosky et al. (2013) rated practice testing and distributed practice as broadly useful across the reviewed techniques, but that is not evidence that all other techniques are useless or that retrieval alone creates understanding. The National Academies' *How People Learn II* emphasizes learners, contexts, cultures, prior knowledge, and transfer. A 2025 classroom-applied distributed-practice meta-analysis reports a positive average effect, but notes limited moderator analysis and no retention intervals beyond 42 days in included studies. Freeman et al.'s active-learning meta-analysis concerns undergraduate STEM and must not be generalized numerically to Bangladeshi secondary classrooms.

The Bangladesh UNICEF Innocenti 2026 differentiated-teaching pilot provides more directly relevant context-specific evidence. It suggests that checking current understanding and matching support to learner level may be useful, while group work without individual accountability can fail in mathematics. Its limits and implementation conditions matter. A full Step 5 source register, Bangla-language search, and claim-by-claim synthesis remain required.

## Step 6. Child & Adolescent Development — preliminary research questions

The education system should account for changes in cognition, language, executive function, identity, emotion, social relationships, sleep, physical development, and autonomy across childhood and adolescence. Development is not a single universal timetable: age-related tendencies coexist with individual, cultural, health, and environmental differences. A rigid age rule can ignore readiness, but unrestricted individualized progression can complicate staffing, peer relationships, curriculum sequence, and credential comparability.

The eventual design should not use simplistic “learning styles” categories or assume that a developmental claim implies one classroom method. Research should distinguish biological development, educational opportunity, prior knowledge, and effects of schooling. Bangladesh-specific questions include nutrition and health, language of instruction, school transitions, adolescent responsibilities, safety, and variation in access to early learning. Recommendations require stronger age-specific sources and contextual evidence than this initial discovery pass contains.

## Step 7. Motivation, Engagement & Independence — preliminary synthesis

Motivation includes interest, perceived value, expectancy of success, belonging, autonomy, habits, goals, and the effects of rewards or sanctions. Neither “intrinsic motivation is always enough” nor “external rewards always work” is a defensible universal rule. Learners can value demanding work and still need structure, practice, feedback, and accountability. Persistent humiliation, arbitrary punishment, meaningless workload, or repeated failure can undermine participation; high expectations with usable support can differ from pressure without support.

A future framework should measure more than attendance or visible compliance. Useful indicators may include persistence on challenging tasks, willingness to seek help, independent planning, engagement, belonging, and ability to recover from mistakes. Claims about motivation interventions must be evaluated for age, context, duration, and unintended effects; correlation between motivation and achievement does not by itself establish causal direction.

## Step 8. Individual Differences & Student Diversity — preliminary synthesis

Learners differ in prior knowledge, pace, language, disability, health, interests, resources, family obligations, confidence, and access to learning materials. Equity does not mean assuming identical needs; it also does not mean assigning students to fixed ability identities. Differentiation must preserve access to ambitious knowledge while providing scaffolding and accessible routes.

The 2026 Bangladesh differentiated-classroom pilot supports further study of diagnostic checks and level-appropriate materials, but it does not settle how to scale differentiation nationally. The research must include disability rights and accessibility, rural and urban differences, socioeconomic barriers, multilingual learners, and learners who are advanced as well as those who need foundational support. Avoid using group averages to make deterministic claims about individual students.

# Phase 3 — Understanding Existing Education

## Step 9. History of Formal Education — research questions

The modern school system combines older religious, civic, guild, colonial, industrial, nation-building, and bureaucratic traditions. Grades, timetables, subject divisions, teacher authority, attendance rules, and standardized examinations evolved for multiple reasons: administration, mass provision, common standards, selection, and control. A historical explanation must not reduce the whole system to a single “factory model” story or infer present-day purpose solely from origins.

For Bangladesh, the history must cover precolonial and religious education, colonial-era institutions and examination structures, Pakistan-era policy, post-1971 nation-building, the 1974 Qudrat-e-Khuda Commission, the 1990s/2000s policy history, the 2010 National Education Policy, 2012 curriculum, and the 2021 framework and later revisions. Each date and policy status needs primary documentary support and a clear account of continuity, change, and contested interpretation.

## Step 10. Anatomy of the Modern Education System — preliminary map

School outcomes emerge from interacting institutions: ministries, curriculum authorities, assessment bodies, school management, teachers, students, families, tutoring providers, publishers, technology providers, universities, employers, regulators, and communities. Incentives may conflict. A teacher may be expected to teach broadly but judged by examination results; a parent may value understanding but fear a child will lose access to a selective pathway without high marks; a school may prioritize reputation; a university may need scalable selection.

The next synthesis should map decision rights, funding, information flows, accountability, and feedback loops. It must separate public, private, general, madrasa, technical/vocational, non-formal, and higher-education pathways rather than treating Bangladesh's system as one homogeneous school.

## Step 11. Problems, Trade-offs & Unintended Consequences — preliminary synthesis

Potential problems to test include weak foundational learning, test washback, unequal access to tutoring, overloaded curricula, rote-only teaching, low feedback quality, exclusion, credential inflation, student stress, poor practical opportunities, fragmented governance, and inconsistent implementation. Each requires evidence; a list of plausible concerns is not proof that all are widespread or equally severe.

Trade-offs include comparability versus breadth, national standards versus local adaptation, curriculum ambition versus instructional time, continuous assessment versus teacher workload, practical learning versus resource costs, and flexibility versus fairness in progression. Any diagnosis should compare affected groups, mechanisms, magnitude, evidence quality, and possible unintended consequences.

## Step 12. What Existing Systems Get Right — preliminary synthesis

Existing systems can provide universal access, shared knowledge, literacy and numeracy instruction, trained adults, peer interaction, routine, safeguarding, recognized qualifications, progression pathways, public accountability, and opportunities for social mobility. Examinations can make some outcomes visible and support selection; common curricula can protect students from a narrow local offering; subject disciplines preserve cumulative knowledge; teachers can identify misconceptions that automated systems miss.

The proposal must identify which practices work, for whom, under what conditions, and at what cost. Reform should preserve demonstrated value and improve weak points rather than equate “traditional” with “bad” or “new” with “effective.”

# Phase 4 — Comparative & International Evidence

## Step 13. Comparative Education Systems — preliminary synthesis

Comparisons should be selected for explanatory value, not prestige. Candidate cases include Finland (teacher professionalism and local curriculum work), Singapore (strong academic outcomes and system alignment), Japan (curriculum coherence and classroom practice), Estonia (digital and curricular development), and selected lower-resource systems with relevant constraints. PISA is useful but narrow: it measures particular competencies in participating populations, not the total purpose or quality of an education system.

PISA 2022 results show strong performance in Singapore and Japan on tested domains, but the findings must be interpreted with sampling, socioeconomic context, well-being, equity, tutoring, curriculum, and teacher-system factors in view. Bangladesh should not be assigned a PISA score when it did not participate in that cycle. Comparisons must not treat an entire country as one intervention or attribute outcomes to a single policy without causal evidence.

## Step 14. Curriculum & School Models — preliminary synthesis

Subject-based curricula can build coherent disciplinary knowledge and progression. Competency-based models can make intended application and performance more explicit but can become vague or administratively burdensome if competencies are poorly defined. Interdisciplinary projects can help connect ideas but may not replace systematic instruction in foundational concepts. Centralization can promote common entitlements and comparability; decentralization can improve responsiveness but amplify capacity gaps.

The research should compare the model actually implemented, not its label. For each model, document curriculum time, teacher preparation, instructional materials, assessment alignment, costs, outcomes, and implementation fidelity. Hybrid approaches may be more realistic than a binary choice between “traditional” and “modern.”

## Step 15. Assessment & Examination Models — preliminary synthesis

Standardized examinations offer scalable comparison and can support certification and selection, but validity depends on the construct measured and the use made of scores. Coursework, oral assessment, projects, portfolios, demonstrations, practical tasks, and teacher judgment can reveal different competencies but may increase workload, inconsistency, bias, and opportunities for unequal support. Continuous assessment is not automatically more valid or less stressful.

The likely design problem is a portfolio of assessment methods with clear purposes: frequent low-stakes checks to guide learning; periodic subject assessments; practical or performance tasks where appropriate; and defensible, transparent certification or selection where necessary. Any proposal must examine reliability, accessibility, moderation, security, teacher workload, coaching incentives, and appeal procedures.

## Step 16. Educational Reform: Successes, Failures & Lessons — preliminary synthesis

Reform often fails when a new curriculum is announced without enough time, teacher support, materials, assessment alignment, community understanding, or implementation monitoring. A policy can be sound in theory and fail in practice; an implementation problem does not automatically disprove the underlying idea. Rapid reversals can also impose costs and undermine trust, but continuity should not be used as an excuse to retain demonstrably ineffective practices.

The Bangladesh 2021 National Curriculum Framework states that curriculum review and needs analysis were conducted and describes a process involving research, situation analysis, working committees, and technical input. This is evidence about the framework's stated development process, not independent proof of effectiveness. A proper reform history must map proposal, approval, piloting, rollout, evaluation, revision, and discontinuation separately, with primary records and independent outcome evidence.

# Phase 5 — Defining the Learner's Journey

## Step 17. Educational Stages & Progression — design questions

A coherent system should define entitlements and aims for early childhood, primary, lower secondary, upper secondary, technical/vocational pathways, and lifelong learning. Stage boundaries should reflect learning progression and practical administration, not merely inherit historical divisions. Transitions are high-risk points for gaps, dropout, identity changes, and pathway inequality.

Before specifying ages or grade structures, compare developmental evidence, foundational learning, staffing, school access, transportation, costs, and national qualifications. Any recommended stage model must be feasible for rural and urban contexts and for different delivery systems.

## Step 18. Age, Readiness & Progression — design questions

Age-based cohorts simplify staffing and peer grouping; mastery-based progression can prevent learners from advancing with serious gaps but may increase costs, stigma, and bottlenecks. Time-on-task matters, but a fixed calendar cannot guarantee mastery. A practical system may combine age-based grouping with targeted remediation, flexible enrichment, transparent progression criteria, and safeguards against indefinite retention.

Research must examine evidence on grade repetition, acceleration, remediation, and mixed-age learning. Avoid presenting mastery progression as a costless solution or assuming every student can progress at an individually unique pace without staffing consequences.

## Step 19. Core Knowledge vs Choice — preliminary synthesis

Learners need a shared foundation: literacy, numeracy, scientific and historical knowledge, languages, civic and ethical understanding, digital and information literacy, health, and practical competence. Yet excessive compulsory breadth can reduce depth, and early specialization can close options prematurely. The right balance depends on stage, curriculum load, social aims, and later pathway requirements.

The project should identify a defensible core through explicit criteria—importance for later learning, civic participation, personal agency, transfer, cultural context, and opportunity costs—rather than a list driven by prestige or current labor-market fashion. Meaningful choice should expand as readiness and specialization needs increase, with guidance and pathways that remain permeable.

## Step 20. Academic, Technical & Vocational Pathways — preliminary synthesis

Academic and vocational routes should offer different forms of depth without encoding a permanent hierarchy of human worth. Technical education needs equipment, qualified instructors, employer links, recognized standards, safe practice, and routes to further learning. Academic pathways also need practical application and should not be treated as purely theoretical.

The system should allow transitions between routes and avoid tracking disadvantaged students into lower-status options by default. Evidence is needed on Bangladesh's labor market, credential recognition, technical/vocational outcomes, employer demand, and student preferences. Forecasts about “future jobs” should be treated as uncertain rather than precise long-term predictions.

# Phase 6 — Curriculum Architecture

## Step 21. Curriculum Design Principles — preliminary synthesis

A curriculum should be coherent, cumulative, age-appropriate, manageable, culturally and linguistically accessible, evidence-informed, and aligned with assessment and teacher capacity. It should distinguish broad aims, subject concepts, learning progressions, examples, practice, and assessable outcomes. A curriculum is not merely a list of chapters or competencies.

The research should test the balance between breadth and depth, repetition and progression, national consistency and local relevance, and knowledge and application. Each major design choice should identify displaced content, implementation requirements, and how success would be measured.

## Step 22. Core Subject Domains — preliminary synthesis

Mathematics, natural sciences, languages, humanities, social sciences, computing, arts, physical education, health, and civic/ethical learning each contribute distinct capabilities. Their time allocations should be justified by stage and purpose, not by tradition alone. Language policy is particularly important in Bangladesh: mother-tongue learning, Bangla, English, and other languages serve different functions and should not be treated as interchangeable.

Disciplinary knowledge is essential to meaningful critical thinking: students cannot reliably evaluate claims about a domain without some knowledge of it. At the same time, factual recall alone is not the whole goal. Subject-domain research should specify concepts, practices, and ways of reasoning, and should consult subject-specific standards and learning research.

## Step 23. Practical, Interdisciplinary & Real-World Learning — preliminary synthesis

Projects, laboratories, fieldwork, demonstrations, and real-world problems can support application, curiosity, collaboration, and integration. Their effectiveness depends on task quality, teacher guidance, prior knowledge, access to equipment, feedback, and individual accountability. A poorly structured project can become a division-of-labor exercise in which some students do little learning.

Practical learning should complement—not automatically replace—explicit teaching and deliberate practice. For low-resource settings, investigate low-cost experiments, locally available materials, simulations, school-community projects, and safe fieldwork. Measure individual understanding as well as the quality of group output.

## Step 24. Life Skills, Communication & Future Capabilities — preliminary synthesis

Financial literacy, communication, collaboration, digital and information literacy, critical evaluation, problem-solving, decision-making, self-management, health, career exploration, and civic participation may be important outcomes. These labels need operational definitions; a “critical thinking” module without subject knowledge or opportunities to practice may not transfer. Life skills can be integrated into subjects and authentic contexts, but integration must not make responsibility invisible.

UNICEF Innocenti's 2026 Bangladesh *Micro Skills in Bangladesh Secondary Schools* working paper reports a first-year school-based life-skills pilot across 120 secondary schools, with approximately 16,000 students; 82% reported feeling better prepared for the future, while 57% attended four sessions or fewer. Self-reported preparedness is not the same as independently measured competence or long-term outcomes. The attendance pattern makes implementation and sustained participation central issues. [Source](https://www.unicef.org/innocenti/documents/micro-skills-bangladesh-secondary-schools).

# Phase 7 — Teaching & Learning Environment

## Step 25. Teaching Methodology — preliminary synthesis

There is no credible basis for selecting one method as universally superior across subjects, ages, prior knowledge, learning objectives, and contexts. Direct instruction, guided practice, discussion, inquiry, retrieval, worked examples, collaborative work, and projects can serve different purposes. The meaningful unit of analysis is the match between method, goal, learner, teacher expertise, materials, and assessment—not a pedagogical label.

Evidence-based practice requires explicit attention to what learners already know, what they must learn next, how misconceptions will be detected, how feedback will be used, and whether knowledge persists or transfers. “Active learning” should mean cognitively meaningful participation, not merely movement or group activity.

## Step 26. Classroom, Timetable, Homework & Learning Environment — preliminary synthesis

Time, class size, classroom climate, noise, physical safety, materials, schedule, sleep, homework, and access to quiet study can influence learning. Small classes are not automatically a sufficient intervention: a Campbell review summarized 127 studies across 41 countries, but only ten studies entered its meta-analysis, showing that evidence about class-size effects is methodologically complex. Costs of smaller classes include hiring and facilities, and effects may depend on teacher practice.

Homework can provide practice and extend learning, but volume alone is not quality. A 2026 three-level meta-analysis of 29 studies and 35,702 students reported a small-to-moderate positive association between teacher homework involvement and outcomes, with stronger associations for homework quality, feedback quality, and autonomy support than feedback quantity. Because the reported result is an association across diverse studies, it should not be interpreted as a guaranteed causal effect of increasing homework. [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2026.100837).

## Step 27. Practical & Project-Based Learning — preliminary synthesis

Projects are most defensible when they have clear learning goals, adequate background knowledge, structured milestones, meaningful feedback, authentic but manageable problems, and a way to assess each learner's contribution and understanding. They are less defensible when the project goal is vague, resources are unequal, or evaluation rewards presentation polish over learning.

For Bangladesh, implementation must account for crowded classes, uneven laboratory resources, teacher workload, exam alignment, safety, and rural access. A tiered model—simple low-cost practical tasks for all, more resource-intensive work where feasible, and shared facilities where needed—deserves investigation. It should be tested for equity rather than assumed to be equitable.

# Phase 8 — Assessment & Examinations

## Step 28. Purpose and Design of Assessment — preliminary synthesis

Assessment can diagnose learning, guide instruction, motivate effort, certify attainment, select applicants, monitor systems, and provide public accountability. These purposes are not interchangeable. An assessment designed to rank candidates may not provide the most useful feedback for teaching; a low-stakes classroom check may not be secure or comparable enough for certification.

Validity is about whether evidence supports a particular interpretation and use of scores. Fairness includes accessibility, construct-irrelevant barriers, consistent administration, moderation, and the consequences of errors. Assessment design should begin with the decision to be made and the construct to be measured, then select a suitable method.

## Step 29. Formative, Summative & Practical Assessment — preliminary synthesis

Formative assessment is useful when information changes what teacher or learner does next. It is not simply frequent grading. Summative assessment supports judgments about what has been learned at a point in time. Practical tasks and portfolios can capture performance that written tests miss, but require clear rubrics, authenticity checks, calibration, and manageable workload.

A mixed assessment architecture may be appropriate: regular low-stakes diagnostic checks; subject tests that include both knowledge and reasoning; demonstrations or practical tasks where warranted; and moderated certification. Its success depends on teacher training, time, material access, transparent standards, and protections against bias or inflated marks.

### Bangladesh-specific evidence surfaced during the second search pass

A 2020 pilot study of examination-related stress surveyed 210 tertiary-level youths aged 17–25 in Dhaka using an online cross-sectional design and a modified DASS-21. Its sample is small, geographically limited, tertiary-level, and non-probability; it cannot establish prevalence among all Bangladeshi school students or prove that examinations alone caused the reported symptoms. A separate cross-sectional online study of 452 HSC graduates seeking university admission reported substantial depression, anxiety, and stress symptom levels, but the admission-seeking sample and design also limit generalization and causal inference. These studies justify treating student well-being and high-stakes selection as research questions—not using their percentages as national prevalence estimates. Sources: [Arusha & Biswas (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.childyouth.2020.105254); [admission-seeking students study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37181689/).

For disability inclusion, a 2024 analysis using Bangladesh MICS 2019 data included 5,024 children with disabilities aged 6–17 and examined factors associated with non-attendance. This is more useful for population-level association than a small convenience sample, but it remains cross-sectional and cannot by itself establish causal effects. A 2025 qualitative study of government primary teachers identifies training and infrastructure barriers; a 2023 secondary-school study used ten schools from Dhaka and Rajshahi selected by convenience. These studies differ in design and population and should not be pooled as though they measure the same outcome. Sources: [MICS-based study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11501013/); [Siddik (2025)](https://doi.org/10.32204/jids.33.3_61); [Zaman & Raqib (2023)](https://doi.org/10.3329/twjer.v48i1.67545).

A Bangladesh parliamentary budget briefing reports FY2024–25 proposed education-sector allocations and distinguishes the proposed budget from revised prior-year allocations. Budget allocation alone is not evidence of actual expenditure, adequate per-student funding, or effectiveness; the final analysis should compare executed spending, price changes, enrollment, distribution, and outcomes. Official budget data are available from the [Ministry of Education's Secondary and Higher Education Division](https://shed.gov.bd/pages/static-pages/694146bea31054345f0fcaeb) and the [Parliament budget briefing](https://www.parliament.gov.bd/uploads/pages/budget-helpdesk-2024_015940_%E0%A6%B6%E0%A6%BF%E0%A6%95%E0%A7%8D%E0%A6%B7%E0%A6%BE.pdf).

## Step 30. Examinations, Grades & High-Stakes Decisions — preliminary synthesis

Examinations can measure some knowledge and skills reliably, provide a common signal, and support scalable selection. They can also narrow curriculum, reward coaching, encourage strategic omission, and turn marks into a proxy for personal worth when stakes are high. Whether an exam is harmful depends on its design, stakes, alignment, preparation inequalities, and downstream decisions.

The goal should not be to abolish exams by default. It should be to specify what they are good for, what they miss, how much weight they should carry, how to reduce avoidable washback, and what additional evidence is required for high-stakes decisions. The credential-pressure pilot remains unapproved and needs Bangladesh-specific current evidence on admissions, hiring, coaching, and exam specifications before it can support major recommendations.

# Phase 9 — Teachers, Technology & AI

## Step 31. Teacher Role, Preparation & Professional Development — preliminary synthesis

Teachers do more than deliver content: they explain, diagnose, model disciplinary reasoning, organize practice, give feedback, maintain a safe environment, motivate, adapt instruction, assess, and support development. Accountability systems that focus narrowly on examination scores can undermine these functions or encourage teaching to the measured construct. Conversely, professional autonomy without adequate preparation, resources, support, or accountability may produce inconsistent opportunities.

Teacher reform must consider initial preparation, subject knowledge, coaching/mentoring, continuous professional development, workload, pay, class size, leadership, materials, career pathways, and working conditions. Training alone is not enough if the system does not allow teachers to use what they learned.

## Step 32. Technology in Education — preliminary synthesis

Technology can widen access to resources, support feedback, accessibility, simulation, communication, and administration. It can also distract, create costs, expose personal data, amplify unequal access, or digitize weak pedagogy without improving learning. “Digital” is not an outcome. The correct comparison is what technology changes relative to a realistic alternative, for whom, at what total cost, and with what risks.

Bangladesh-specific implementation must include device access, connectivity, electricity, Bangla content quality, maintenance, teacher confidence, privacy, and offline alternatives. The UNESCO 2023 Global Education Monitoring report argues for evidence-based, appropriate use of technology and emphasizes that technology should serve learners and teachers rather than be treated as an end in itself. [UNESCO GEM report](https://www.unesco.org/gem-report/en/technology).

## Step 33. Artificial Intelligence & the Future of Learning — preliminary synthesis

AI may support explanation, practice, translation, accessibility, formative feedback, and teacher preparation. Risks include confident errors, fabricated citations, privacy violations, overdependence, bias, cheating, unequal access, reduced human interaction, and systems that optimize engagement rather than learning. The value depends on task design, age, data governance, verification, teacher oversight, and the learner's ability to evaluate outputs.

UNESCO's guidance on generative AI recommends human-centred policy, data privacy protection, age-appropriate use, and ethical validation. These are policy principles, not proof that a particular AI product improves learning. A future system should preserve non-AI routes, protect minors, make limitations visible, and evaluate outcomes against a credible comparison rather than counting usage. [UNESCO guidance](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/guidance-generative-ai-education-and-research).

# Phase 10 — Equity, Governance & System Conditions

## Step 34. Equity, Accessibility & Student Protection — preliminary synthesis

Equity requires examining who receives access, instructional time, qualified teachers, learning materials, language support, safety, disability accommodations, and fair assessment. National averages can hide large differences by geography, household resources, school type, disability, gender-related barriers, and language. Equality of provision is not always equality of opportunity, but targeted support must be transparent and avoid stigma.

Student protection includes physical and emotional safety, privacy, anti-bullying measures, safeguarding, accessible complaints, and appropriate adult conduct. Any original research involving minors would require checking local law and ethics requirements, institutional review where applicable, guardian consent and child assent, data minimization, anonymization, and secure retention. The present document-based research should not pretend to have interviewed students.

## Step 35. Families, School Leadership & Governance — preliminary synthesis

Families influence attendance, study conditions, expectations, tutoring, language exposure, and decisions about pathways. Schools need leadership that supports teaching quality, teacher learning, safe environments, and transparent use of assessment. Government sets entitlements, curriculum, standards, financing, and accountability; local discretion can improve responsiveness but may widen inequality when capacity differs.

A workable governance model needs clear decision rights, transparent policy records, stable but revisable commitments, stakeholder consultation, reliable data, and routes to challenge harmful implementation. Educational aims should not be rewritten solely to mirror party changes; however, claims about political motive require direct evidence, not chronology. The analysis should separate legitimate democratic disagreement about values from partisan manipulation of curriculum or historical content.

## Step 36. Resources, Funding & Low-Resource Implementation — preliminary synthesis

Every proposal has recurring costs: teachers, training, salaries, materials, buildings, transport, connectivity, devices, assessment, maintenance, monitoring, and opportunity costs. A model that works only in well-funded urban schools is not a national design. Cost estimates should identify one-time and recurrent expenses, staff time, capital requirements, cost per learner, and the likely source of funds.

The system should define a minimum viable entitlement that is realistic in resource-constrained contexts, then show how quality can improve as capacity grows. Low-cost should not mean lower expectations for disadvantaged students. Offline and locally available alternatives should be planned from the beginning, and proposed pilots should include stop/go criteria and measures of unintended burden.

# Phase 11 — Education Beyond School

## Step 37. Higher Education, Careers & Workforce Transition — preliminary synthesis

School education should help students understand options and develop transferable foundations, but should not pretend to predict exact future jobs. Labor markets change; forecasts are uncertain; employer demands are not the only legitimate purpose of education. Career guidance should include academic, technical, vocational, entrepreneurial, public-service, caregiving, creative, and lifelong-learning pathways.

The project needs Bangladesh-specific evidence on employment transitions, graduate outcomes, skill mismatches, technical/vocational status, and the role of credentials in hiring. Avoid claiming that one course or digital skill guarantees employment. Stronger pathways require employer feedback, recognized qualifications, career information, opportunities to retrain, and the ability to move between education and work.

## Step 38. Citizenship, Society & Lifelong Learning — preliminary synthesis

Education can support informed civic participation, critical engagement with information, pluralism, social responsibility, cultural understanding, environmental awareness, and the ability to keep learning. These aims require careful treatment in a diverse society: civic education should not become partisan indoctrination, and pluralism does not require pretending that all claims are equally supported by evidence.

Lifelong learning depends on literacy, curiosity, confidence, accessible learning opportunities, and the ability to evaluate information. The framework should include the foundations for continued learning while acknowledging that schooling cannot alone solve wider social, political, or economic problems.

# Phase 12 — Designing, Testing & Defending the Proposed System

## Step 39. Complete Proposed Education System — not yet designed or approved

The proposed system must be derived only after the earlier evidence is appraised. Its architecture should specify aims, stages, progression, core knowledge, pathways, curriculum, teaching, assessment, teacher preparation, technology, governance, equity safeguards, funding, and evaluation. Every major component should trace to evidence, a stated value premise, a design decision, an expected outcome, and a possible failure mode.

No complete replacement system is being declared here. The provisional principles in this dossier are candidate hypotheses for later comparison, not a finalized design. A proposal must show what it retains from existing systems, what it changes, why, for whom, under what conditions, and what evidence could falsify its rationale.

## Step 40. Implementation, Evaluation, Stress Testing & Final Framework — not yet designed or approved

The final phase should build an implementation sequence, not a launch-day fantasy. Possible stages include document and stakeholder validation, low-risk prototype pilots, baseline measurement, teacher preparation, controlled rollout, independent evaluation, public reporting, and revision. The sequence must specify budgets, responsible institutions, prerequisites, failure criteria, and how the system would avoid disrupting learners during transition.

Stress tests should include: rural low-connectivity schools; large classes; teacher vacancies; political change; low public funding; weak assessment integrity; private tutoring expansion; AI misinformation; language and disability barriers; family resistance; exam and university admission demands; and failure of a pilot to improve learning. Evaluation should use multiple outcomes—foundational learning, retention, transfer, attendance, equity, well-being, practical capability, teacher workload, and cost—not one score alone. Final publication requires independent review and explicit author approval.

1. What do Bangladeshi students know and do at different grades, using original NSA and other validated learning-assessment data?
2. How do the 2010 policy, 2012 curriculum, 2021 framework, subsequent revisions, examination blueprints, and classroom practices align or diverge?
3. What is the current prevalence, cost, and distribution of coaching and private tutoring by grade, subject, region, and household income?
4. Which assessment changes improve learning without making grading less reliable or increasing inequality?
5. Which reforms have robust causal evidence, and which are mainly supported by descriptive or self-reported results?
6. What are the minimum staffing, training, material, and financial requirements for implementation at national scale?
7. How should learning goals be balanced when values conflict and instructional time is limited?
8. What evidence would cause the project to reject or substantially revise its preferred design?
9. How can pluralism, religious and moral development, climate awareness, and civic education be handled fairly and transparently?

# Provisional design principles to test—not final recommendations

- Define intended learning outcomes and assess the construct that is actually claimed.
- Protect foundational literacy, numeracy, language, and subject knowledge.
- Teach for durable learning and application, not only immediate test performance.
- Combine explicit instruction, guided practice, feedback, retrieval, and authentic application where evidence supports the match.
- Use assessment for diagnosis and improvement as well as certification and selection.
- Align exam incentives with the curriculum's stated aims.
- Support teachers with time, materials, subject knowledge, feedback, and ongoing professional learning.
- Provide accessible, differentiated support without permanently labelling learners.
- Preserve respected academic, technical, and vocational routes with movement between them.
- Treat technology and AI as tools subject to evidence, privacy, safety, cost, and human oversight.
- Design for low-resource contexts and measure opportunity costs.
- Separate empirical claims from value choices and preserve reasonable disagreement.
- Evaluate pilots with transparent baseline, outcome, equity, cost, and failure criteria.
- Publish uncertainty and counterevidence rather than hiding them.
