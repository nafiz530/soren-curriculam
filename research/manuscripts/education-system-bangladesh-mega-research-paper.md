# Education System of Bangladesh
## Is Our Education System Preparing Students for the Future—or Mainly Preparing Them to Pass Examinations?

**A structured evidence review and a proposal for a learning-centred education system that develops the next generation of Bangladeshi leaders**

**Author:** Md. Nafiz  
**Date:** 28 October 2026  
**Document type:** Research paper / policy and system-design proposal  
**Keywords:** Bangladesh education; learning outcomes; examinations; private tutoring; curriculum reform; foundational literacy; numeracy; equity; teacher development; future-ready learning; leadership

---

## Abstract

Education is expected to do more than help a student pass an examination. It should help children read with understanding, reason with evidence, solve unfamiliar problems, work with others, act responsibly and continue learning as society changes. Bangladesh has expanded access to schooling and has repeatedly revised its curriculum, but access, curriculum ambition and examination results do not automatically establish that students are acquiring these capabilities. This paper asks whether Bangladesh's education system is preparing students for life and work or placing too much weight on examination performance.

The paper uses a structured narrative review of government assessment reports, policy documents, civil-society education studies, international indicators and selected comparative evidence. It is not a systematic review, and it does not claim exhaustive coverage. Three findings stand out. First, Bangladesh's National Student Assessment 2022 shows a mixed picture in foundational learning: grade-level attainment improved between 2017 and 2022 in three of four reported grade-subject comparisons, while Grade 5 mathematics fell from 32% to 30%. Second, private tutoring and commercial guidebooks are widespread in the populations covered by Education Watch research, representing both a household-cost burden and a signal that many families seek learning support beyond ordinary classroom provision. Third, repeated curriculum and assessment reversals make implementation, teacher preparation and policy continuity as important as the written curriculum itself.

The evidence does not justify the claim that every Bangladeshi school teaches only for examinations, nor does it establish that examinations are inherently harmful. Examinations can certify learning and provide common standards; the problem arises when a narrow set of high-stakes measures becomes the dominant signal of educational success. The paper proposes a staged, Bangladesh-adapted model that combines strong foundational learning, deep subject knowledge, application and inquiry, practical and digital capability, wellbeing, civic responsibility, flexible pathways and fair assessment. It recommends piloting the model before national expansion, with transparent outcome measures, independent evaluation, teacher support and explicit equity safeguards.

**Conclusion:** Bangladesh does not need another curriculum slogan alone. It needs a stable and adequately supported learning system in which what is taught, what is assessed, how teachers work and what families can afford all point towards meaningful learning.

## 1. Introduction: What should education prepare a child to become?

A student can earn high marks and still struggle to explain an idea in their own words, apply mathematics to a household decision, evaluate an online claim, collaborate on a project or recover after making a mistake. Another student may have practical ability and curiosity but perform poorly in a timed examination. These examples do not prove that examinations are useless. They show why examination performance and educational readiness are related but different questions.

Bangladesh has legitimate reasons to care about examinations. Public examinations can provide a common qualification, support selection into further study and create a visible goal for students and families. In a country where access to competitive educational and employment opportunities is uneven, families may reasonably treat certificates and marks as consequential. Teachers also work under curriculum, time, class-size, resource and accountability constraints. It is therefore too simple to blame students, teachers or parents for responding to the incentives that the system creates.

The central question is not whether students should learn the syllabus or whether examinations should exist. Students need knowledge, and credible assessments matter. The question is whether the system gives sufficient weight to the full set of capabilities that students need after the examination: understanding, retention, reasoning, communication, practical competence, ethical judgement, adaptability and the ability to learn independently.

Bangladesh's own policy documents recognise a broader ambition. The National Education Policy 2010 set out objectives extending beyond passing examinations. The National Curriculum Framework 2021 likewise described a need for a more flexible curriculum capable of responding to changing social and economic needs. Its introductory discussion explicitly criticised the historical dominance of memorisation and examination orientation (Ministry of Education, 2010; National Curriculum and Textbook Board [NCTB], 2021). Yet a policy document is evidence of intent, not proof of classroom implementation.

The implementation context has also been unsettled. Bangladesh introduced a competency-oriented curriculum in phases beginning in 2023, but the secondary curriculum was rolled back towards the 2012 framework under the interim administration in 2025, while the future direction of revision remained under discussion (United Nations Population Fund [UNFPA], 2025; Government of Bangladesh, 2025). This sequence matters because teachers, students, families, publishers and institutions need to understand what counts as good learning and how it will be assessed. Repeated changes can create uncertainty, especially when training, materials and assessment systems do not move together.

This paper evaluates the question through evidence rather than assuming a preferred answer. It aims to:

1. describe what selected national data say about foundational learning;
2. assess what tutoring, guidebook use and curriculum changes can—and cannot—tell us about examination pressure;
3. compare Bangladesh's current arrangements with selected international evidence without treating any country as a ready-made template;
4. propose a realistic education model from early childhood through secondary, technical and higher education; and
5. set out a pilot and evaluation strategy that can test whether the proposal improves learning and opportunity.

The phrase “the perfect education system” is best treated as an aspiration, not a literal design claim. No single model is perfect for every child, district, school or labour market. A more defensible goal is an education system that learns from evidence, corrects its own failures, protects every child's access to foundational learning and helps students develop the knowledge and agency to shape Bangladesh's future.

## 2. Review approach and limits

### 2.1 Type of review

This is a **structured narrative evidence review with realist-informed reasoning**. It brings together national assessment results, policy texts, Education Watch findings, international monitoring sources and selected comparative evidence to examine how educational incentives may work in the Bangladeshi context. A realist-informed approach asks not only whether a policy is described as successful, but what mechanism might produce a result, for which students, under which conditions.

This is not a systematic review or meta-analysis. The searches documented for this project were exploratory and targeted; they were not exhaustive, and not every relevant Bangla-language database or institutional search interface has been searched. There was no registered protocol, comprehensive dual screening, formal risk-of-bias synthesis across all studies or PRISMA flow. The findings should therefore be read as an evidence-informed assessment, not a complete census of the literature.

### 2.2 Sources and selection

Priority was given to sources that could directly inform the Bangladesh question:

- the Directorate of Primary Education's National Student Assessment (NSA) 2022 report;
- the World Bank's 2024 Bangladesh Learning Poverty Brief;
- Education Watch reports coordinated by the Campaign for Popular Education (CAMPE);
- the National Education Policy 2010 and National Curriculum Framework 2021;
- official or intergovernmental material on curriculum, assessment and education technology;
- selected OECD comparative indicators, particularly the Estonia PISA 2022 country note; and
- research and review literature on assessment, learning strategies and formative feedback.

Official primary reports were preferred for quantitative claims. News reports were used as discovery or corroborating sources where they summarised a report, not as a substitute for primary data where the original report was available. Numbers from different studies were not combined when the populations, years or definitions were materially different.

### 2.3 How claims are judged

The paper distinguishes five kinds of statement:

- **Descriptive:** a source reports a value or policy decision.
- **Associational:** two factors are related, but a causal effect has not been established.
- **Causal:** a design supports a causal interpretation under stated assumptions.
- **Interpretive:** a plausible explanation of how incentives or institutions may operate.
- **Normative or proposed:** a judgement about what the system should value, or a design recommendation to test.

The terms *supported*, *plausible*, *contested* and *unknown* are used qualitatively and relative to the scope of a claim. They are not a formal GRADE rating. Where evidence is incomplete, the paper says so rather than filling the gap with a confident-sounding estimate.

### 2.4 Limitations

The review has four important limits. First, the evidence base is stronger for primary-level Bangla and mathematics than for secondary-level reasoning, higher education readiness, technical and vocational outcomes, student wellbeing or long-term employment. Second, some tutoring figures come from particular years and populations and cannot be treated as a single continuous trend. Third, the review does not include original interviews with students, parents, teachers, employers or policymakers. Fourth, it has not yet completed the proposed systematic coding of SSC/HSC examination questions. As a result, the paper can discuss incentives and design risks, but it cannot quantify the proportion of current public-examination marks devoted to recall versus higher-order thinking.

These limits narrow the claims; they do not make the question unimportant. They also define the next research priorities.

## 3. What counts as future-ready education?

“Future-ready” is often used as a slogan. For it to guide policy, it needs a practical definition. In this paper, future readiness means the capacity to use knowledge responsibly in changing situations while retaining the foundations needed for further learning. It does not mean replacing academic subjects with vague “soft skills,” nor does it mean predicting every future job.

A future-ready student should develop at least eight connected dimensions.

1. **Foundational literacy and numeracy.** Read with comprehension, write clearly, interpret information, estimate, calculate and reason quantitatively.
2. **Deep subject knowledge.** Build coherent knowledge in Bangla, English, mathematics, science, social sciences, arts and other subjects. Application without knowledge can become superficial.
3. **Reasoning and transfer.** Explain why an answer is correct, compare alternatives, use evidence, identify assumptions and apply learning to a new but appropriate problem.
4. **Communication and collaboration.** Listen, ask useful questions, present an argument, write for an audience, contribute to a group and resolve disagreement constructively.
5. **Practical and digital capability.** Use tools safely, evaluate digital information, understand basic data and financial decisions, make or repair things where relevant, and recognise the limits of technology.
6. **Self-regulation and wellbeing.** Plan work, practise effectively, seek help, respond to feedback, manage stress and understand health and safety.
7. **Ethical, civic and environmental judgement.** Consider consequences, distinguish evidence from rumour, understand rights and responsibilities, and participate constructively in community life.
8. **Agency and lifelong learning.** Set goals, explore interests, reflect on mistakes and continue learning beyond formal schooling.

These dimensions should not be treated as eight separate subjects competing for timetable space. They can be developed through ordinary academic learning. A science lesson can require accurate subject knowledge, a written explanation, data interpretation, a small investigation and a discussion of environmental consequences. A mathematics lesson can include procedural fluency, estimation, a real-life problem and an explanation of strategy. A history lesson can teach factual chronology and ask students to compare sources, identify perspective and build an evidence-based argument.

The system should also recognise that children develop at different rates. Early grades require secure foundations and structured support, not premature pressure to produce sophisticated projects. Older students can gradually take on more complex inquiry, independent work, career exploration and civic participation. “Future-ready” is therefore a developmental progression, not a demand that every child perform like an adult.

## 4. Bangladesh's learning reality: what the available numbers say

### 4.1 National Student Assessment 2022

The NSA is a sample-based national assessment of students in Grades 3 and 5 in Bangla and mathematics. It is designed to describe performance at national level, not to provide an individual score for every child. The 2022 round covered a large national sample and was the first national primary learning assessment after the COVID-19 disruption (Directorate of Primary Education [DPE], 2023; Australian Council for Educational Research, 2023).

The report defines “grade-level competency” as students in the **Proficient** and **Advanced** performance levels. The figures below are the proportions meeting that definition.

The data do not support a one-sided story. Grade-level attainment rose in three comparisons and fell in Grade 5 mathematics. But even after the reported improvements, approximately half or more of the assessed students in three comparisons did not meet the report's grade-level standard; in Grade 5 mathematics, around seven in ten did not. These results should focus attention on the quality and consistency of foundational learning, not only on completion rates, curriculum documents or examination pass rates.

The comparison also requires care. The 2022 assessment added pre-reading and early-numeracy items and included geographical type as an additional stratification variable. The report uses a technical assessment framework to support comparisons, but the assessment context is not identical in every respect across rounds. Changes should therefore be described as the report presents them, with methodological caveats, rather than as a perfectly invariant time series.

A further caution is essential: failing to meet the assessment's grade-level standard is not the same as having no skill. Students fall across performance bands, and the report's cut points represent a defined standard. The result is still serious: too many children are not yet reaching the expected level in the foundational subjects on which later learning depends.

### 4.2 Learning poverty: a useful but distinct indicator

The World Bank's *Bangladesh Learning Poverty Brief 2024* reports learning poverty of 51%, learning deprivation of 50% and schooling deprivation of 2.4%. The brief reports 54% learning poverty for boys and 49% for girls. Learning poverty combines reading proficiency and schooling access: it estimates the share of children who cannot read and understand an age-appropriate text by around age ten, including children who are out of school and therefore unlikely to have acquired that skill (World Bank, 2024).

These indicators should not be casually merged with the NSA percentages. The NSA measures performance in Bangla and mathematics in Grades 3 and 5 under its own assessment standards. The learning-poverty estimate is a separate internationally harmonised indicator, with its own data vintage and methodology. A separate World Bank global database workbook updated in 2021 contains historical Bangladesh estimates for 2015 and 2017, but those figures should not be plotted as a direct trend against the 2024 brief's 51% because the assessment inputs and thresholds differ.

The 51% figure is also not a fresh direct test of every ten-year-old in Bangladesh in 2024. It is a brief-specific estimate based on the available underlying data, which the brief describes in the context of the COVID-19 school-closure period. It is best used as a warning indicator, not a real-time scorecard.

### 4.3 What the evidence permits us to conclude

The strongest conclusion is limited but important: foundational learning remains a major national challenge. The evidence does **not** by itself establish why the challenge persists. Possible contributors include teaching time, attendance, household poverty, class conditions, language and learning differences, teacher support, curriculum sequencing, assessment incentives and access to learning materials. Their relative contributions need stronger evidence.

This distinction matters politically and practically. If poor learning is blamed entirely on examination culture, policy may overlook early childhood development, teacher working conditions, poverty, disability inclusion or the availability of books. If it is blamed entirely on family poverty, the system may avoid examining whether classroom instruction and assessment help children learn. A serious diagnosis tests several explanations at once.

## 5. Examinations, tutoring and the incentives around learning

### 5.1 Why examination pressure is rational for families

In a competitive system, examination results can influence access to a preferred school, college, university subject, scholarship or career pathway. A family may therefore invest in private tutoring, guidebooks and repeated practice even when it would prefer a broader education. A teacher may focus on likely examination content because that is what students and parents demand and what is easiest to demonstrate through marks. A student may memorise an answer because it is rewarded reliably under time pressure.

This is an incentive problem, not simply a moral failure. If a system publicly rewards a narrow outcome, it should expect institutions and families to organise around that outcome. Telling students to become creative while selection depends overwhelmingly on a limited examination may produce a mismatch between the official curriculum and rational behaviour.

However, examinations are not automatically the cause of poor learning. Well-designed tests can motivate practice, identify gaps and protect standards. The problem is **misalignment**: when the test samples only a narrow part of learning, teaching and studying may narrow in response. This mechanism is often described as *washback*, meaning the influence of testing on teaching and learning. The strength of washback depends on the test's stakes, content, format, transparency, the resources available to teachers and how results are used.

### 5.2 What tutoring and guidebook figures show

Education Watch research coordinated by CAMPE provides important evidence about how families supplement school learning. Its 2022 study, reported in 2023, found that around 85% of students in Grades 8 and 9 had taken private tuition in the year studied. It also reported that 79% of primary students and 82.5% of secondary students used commercially produced guidebooks or notebooks in 2022 (CAMPE, 2023; The Daily Star, 2023).

The Education Watch 2023 report subsequently reported private tutoring for 70% of primary students and 76.5% of secondary students in its study, and guidebook use of 92% and 93%, respectively. It also reported that average annual household education costs in 2022 were Tk 13,882 for a primary student and Tk 27,340 for a secondary student, with private tutoring and commercial guidebooks/notebooks among the major cost items. Reported family education expenditures in the first half of 2023 rose by 25% at primary level and 51% at secondary level compared with 2022 (CAMPE, 2024).

The pattern is consistent with extensive reliance on support outside ordinary classroom instruction. It does not prove that all tutoring is harmful, that every tutor teaches only for examinations, or that private tutoring alone explains weak learning outcomes. Tutoring can provide additional explanation, practice or individual attention that a crowded classroom may not be able to offer. Guidebooks can also contain useful practice. The policy question is why these products become necessary or nearly universal, who benefits, what families must pay, and whether students without the money receive an adequate alternative.

The distributional consequences are especially important. If a student can access extra instruction, a quiet study space, a personal device and commercial materials while another student cannot, the same public examination may reflect both learning and unequal access to preparation. The system should not assume that a common paper creates a level playing field when preparation conditions differ sharply.

### 5.3 Household cost is a learning and equity issue

Education can be nominally free while imposing substantial household costs. Transport, uniforms, stationery, examination-related costs, tutoring and guidebooks may all matter. CAMPE's reported average costs are not a complete account of every household's spending, but they demonstrate that families can bear significant expenses beyond school fees. Some parents reported that what they could afford was much lower than the average spending reported by the study (CAMPE, 2024).

A reform that introduces project work, digital platforms or continuous assessment without providing materials, devices, teacher time and accessible alternatives could increase inequality rather than reduce it. Students with more resources may purchase better projects, devices or coaching; teachers may face additional marking work; and families may interpret every new activity as another expense. A fair reform must therefore be designed around public provision, not the assumption that households can fill every gap.

### 5.4 What remains unknown

The current evidence base does not yet provide a sufficiently complete, nationally representative causal account of how much examination pressure changes classroom practice, student understanding, stress or later work outcomes. Nor does it provide a complete contemporary estimate of tutoring costs by income group, gender, disability, district and school type. These are important research questions, not details to be filled with guesswork.

A central next step is to code a transparent sample of SSC and HSC examination papers across years and subjects. Items should be coded by the knowledge and thinking they require—recall, explanation, routine application, unfamiliar application, analysis or evaluation—and by marks, not merely by their wording. A second coder should independently code a subset. Until that analysis is completed, this paper does not claim to know what percentage of current examination marks rewards memorisation.

## 6. Curriculum reform and the implementation problem

### 6.1 The gap between written ambition and classroom conditions

The National Curriculum Framework 2021 was designed around a broader view of learning and explicitly identified the limitations of a memorisation- and examination-oriented model (NCTB, 2021). Its ambition included competencies and learning that could respond to changing needs. That direction is broadly consistent with the argument of this paper: education should build knowledge and the ability to use it.

But a curriculum is not a classroom. Implementation requires a coherent chain:

1. the intended learning goals must be clear;
2. textbooks and other materials must support those goals;
3. teachers must understand the content and how to teach it;
4. class sizes, time and resources must allow the intended activities;
5. assessments must reward the intended learning;
6. school leaders and families must understand the expectations; and
7. the system must collect evidence and correct problems without repeatedly destabilising schools.

If one link fails, the reform may be misunderstood or implemented unevenly. For example, project-based learning may become a decorative poster made by a parent rather than an opportunity for a student to investigate, revise and explain. Continuous assessment may become extra paperwork instead of useful feedback. A competency framework may be interpreted as reduced academic rigour if subject knowledge and standards are not explained clearly.

### 6.2 Why policy continuity matters

Bangladesh's curriculum changes illustrate the difference between policy announcement and sustained implementation. The competency-oriented framework introduced from 2023 was followed by major reversals at secondary level in 2025, with a return towards the 2012 curriculum while a revised framework was discussed for later introduction (UNFPA, 2025; Government of Bangladesh, 2025). This is a description of the policy transition documented in the cited sources, not a claim that no further decisions have occurred since then. The exact current rollout status should be rechecked against official notices before publication or implementation.

Frequent change creates costs. Teachers must relearn expectations; textbook writers and assessment bodies must adapt; parents receive conflicting messages; students may experience a curriculum under one framework and a public examination under another. Yet continuity should not mean freezing a flawed curriculum. The goal is **stable direction with evidence-led revision**: preserve agreed long-term learning goals while changing specific practices when evaluation shows that they are not working.

A cross-party, professionally led agreement on foundational standards, assessment principles, teacher development and transparent review cycles would reduce the risk that every political transition restarts the system. The public should be able to see which parts of a reform are retained, changed or abandoned, and why.

### 6.3 The lesson from implementation evidence

Education Watch 2023 reported that teachers faced resource and technology constraints and that centralised management and rigid class routines limited their ability to organise some kinds of group work and projects (CAMPE, 2024). These findings suggest that teachers' capacity to deliver an intended curriculum depends on conditions as well as motivation.

It would be unfair to ask teachers to deliver inquiry, differentiated support, detailed formative feedback and complex projects without time, training, materials or manageable assessment loads. It would also be unfair to students to treat the existence of a new curriculum as proof that those learning experiences are available to them.

## 7. What can be learned from other education systems?

International comparison is useful when it helps identify mechanisms and trade-offs. It is misleading when it becomes a ranking exercise or assumes that a policy can be copied without its supporting institutions.

### 7.1 Estonia: strong performance is not a single-policy story

Estonia is a useful comparator because it has performed above the OECD average in PISA 2022 mathematics, reading and science. In the OECD country note, 85% of Estonian students reached at least Level 2 in mathematics and 90% did so in science. The note also reports that socioeconomic status remains associated with performance, that the mathematics gap between advantaged and disadvantaged students was 81 score points, and that staffing shortages were a reported concern in many schools (OECD, 2023).

The lesson is not that Bangladesh should copy Estonia's curriculum. Estonia's outcomes are produced by a wider institutional context, including teacher professionalism, materials, governance, family conditions and a different economic and demographic setting. Even a high-performing system has weaknesses and inequalities. Its experience suggests that strong foundational standards, teacher support, attention to equity and meaningful autonomy can coexist; it does not prove that any one of these features alone caused the results.

### 7.2 Finland and Singapore: borrow principles, not national mythology

Finland's curriculum framework gives cross-cutting competencies a place across subjects, while Singapore has used policy changes to address assessment load and learning pathways. These are useful policy examples, but a policy page does not establish its effect on achievement. International systems differ in teacher selection and preparation, public finance, class organisation, social protection, language, household income and political continuity.

Bangladesh should therefore ask: what problem was the policy designed to solve? What evidence supports it? What professional capacity and funding did implementation require? Who benefited and who may have lost out? What would have to change for the mechanism to work in a Bangladeshi school? A reform should be adapted only after these questions are answered.

### 7.3 Learning science: the value of practice, feedback and retrieval

Research on learning provides a more transferable set of principles, although implementation still depends on age, subject and context. Retrieval practice—trying to recall knowledge rather than only rereading it—can improve long-term retention when used appropriately (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006). Reviews of learning techniques have found that practice testing and distributed practice are often more useful than strategies such as repeated rereading alone (Dunlosky et al., 2013). Formative assessment can support learning when evidence of understanding is used to adjust teaching and feedback, rather than simply adding marks or paperwork (Black & Wiliam, 1998).

These findings do not imply that every lesson needs a quiz or that students should be tested constantly. Retrieval should be low-stakes and followed by correction; spacing needs a workable sequence; feedback should tell students what to improve and how; and teachers need time to respond to the evidence. The key principle is that practice should make understanding stronger, not merely train students to reproduce an answer that will soon be forgotten.

## 8. The education system Bangladesh should build

The proposal below is a design framework, not a claim that one model has already been proven in Bangladesh. It combines evidence-informed principles with normative choices: every child should gain foundations, subject knowledge, agency and a fair opportunity to contribute. The framework should be piloted and revised with students, teachers, parents, disability advocates, employers, researchers and communities.

### 8.1 Stage 1: Early childhood and pre-primary — build readiness without premature pressure

**Priority:** language development, curiosity, play, movement, social connection, early number sense and a safe learning environment.

Children should encounter stories, conversation, drawing, movement, music, sorting, counting and exploration. Early learning should not become a race to memorise long answers or complete formal worksheets before children are developmentally ready. Families need accessible guidance on talking, reading and playing with children; early childhood centres need safe spaces, trained staff and appropriate materials.

Bangladesh-specific implementation should account for rural access, poverty, nutrition, disability, local languages and the burden on caregivers. A national model must not assume every child has books, internet access or a parent who can provide one-to-one instruction.

**Evidence of progress:** language and communication development, early numeracy, participation, wellbeing, inclusion and transition into primary school—not a high-stakes ranking of very young children.

### 8.2 Stage 2: Primary school — secure the foundations for every learner

**Priority:** every child learns to read with comprehension, write clearly, develop number sense and mathematical reasoning, and build curiosity about the world.

The NSA results make foundational learning the first national priority. Schools should identify learning gaps early and provide additional instruction before gaps become permanent. A child who has not mastered a prerequisite should receive targeted support rather than simply move through the syllabus and encounter the same difficulty in every later grade.

A practical model would include:

- a clear, limited set of grade-level learning standards;
- structured teaching materials and high-quality textbooks;
- daily reading, writing, mathematical reasoning and discussion;
- short, low-stakes checks for understanding;
- flexible small-group support where feasible;
- accessible books and materials for children with disabilities;
- teacher coaching focused on real classroom practice; and
- protected time for learning, with fewer low-value administrative demands.

Students should learn core facts and procedures, but they should also explain their reasoning. Memorisation is useful for vocabulary, multiplication facts, formulas and essential knowledge; it becomes a problem when reproducing information substitutes for understanding how and when to use it.

**Evidence of progress:** repeated, age-appropriate assessments of reading comprehension, writing and numeracy; attendance; learning growth by subgroup; and evidence that students retain and apply what they learned.

### 8.3 Stage 3: Lower secondary — broaden knowledge and discover strengths

**Priority:** maintain strong foundations while helping students explore science, humanities, languages, arts, technology, practical skills and civic life.

At this stage, students should move from mainly guided practice towards increasingly independent work. They should read longer texts, compare explanations, design simple investigations, interpret data, discuss historical and social questions, use digital information safely and learn to explain an answer rather than only state it.

Every project should have a clear learning purpose. A project is not automatically superior to a well-taught lesson. It should require the student to use knowledge, make decisions, receive feedback and explain what was learned. Schools without laboratories or reliable internet should have low-cost alternatives using local materials, paper, observation and community knowledge.

Career awareness should begin as exploration rather than early tracking. Students should encounter examples of technical, academic, creative, public-service, entrepreneurial and care-related work. No pathway should be treated as a destination for students labelled “weak.”

### 8.4 Stage 4: Upper secondary — rigorous knowledge, choice and meaningful assessment

**Priority:** prepare students for higher education, technical training, employment, entrepreneurship and responsible adult life without making one examination the only credible evidence of ability.

Students need depth in chosen subjects and a broad common foundation. Academic and technical pathways should have clear standards, qualified teachers and routes to progress. Students should be able to change direction when they discover a new interest or ability, rather than being permanently restricted by one decision made at age fifteen.

Assessment should combine common examinations with moderated coursework or practical work where feasible. Practical components must be designed so that a student's score reflects their own learning, not a family's ability to buy materials or hire someone to produce the work. Oral explanations, individual reflections, in-class tasks and sampling can help check authenticity.

Public examinations should remain credible and comparable. But the questions should sample a wider range of learning: accurate knowledge, explanation, routine and non-routine application, evidence-based reasoning and extended responses appropriate to the subject. A balanced paper can retain basic questions while ensuring that marks are not dominated by predictable reproduction.

### 8.5 Technical and vocational education — equal dignity, clear standards

Technical and vocational education should be a respected option with real progression, not a lower-status destination. Programmes should be developed with employers, professional bodies, local industries and community needs. Students need practical instruction, safety, foundational mathematics and literacy, digital skills, communication and work-based learning.

Credentials should state what a learner can actually do. Work placements must not become unpaid labour without learning supervision. Programme performance should be measured by completion, demonstrated competence, further learning, employment quality and graduate feedback—not only enrolment numbers.

Bangladesh needs stronger, comparable public evidence on technical graduate outcomes by programme and region. Until such evidence is extracted and appraised, this paper does not claim that a particular vocational model already produces superior employment outcomes nationally.

### 8.6 Higher education and lifelong learning — reward inquiry and public contribution

Universities and colleges should develop disciplinary depth, research literacy, ethical reasoning, professional capability and independent thought. Admission and assessment should recognise prior learning fairly while protecting standards. Research projects, internships, community work and entrepreneurship can be valuable when they are supervised and assessed against transparent criteria.

A future-ready system must also serve people after initial schooling. Adults need accessible opportunities to improve literacy, digital capability, technical skills and professional knowledge. Employers and public institutions should support continuing learning rather than treating education as something that ends at graduation.

### 8.7 Leadership as a developmental outcome

The goal of preparing the next generation of Bangladeshi leaders should not mean training every student to seek political or corporate power. Leadership is the ability to take responsibility, understand a problem, work with others, use evidence, make decisions under uncertainty and remain accountable for consequences. It can be exercised by a teacher, nurse, engineer, farmer, researcher, entrepreneur, public servant, artist or community organiser.

Students should practise leadership in age-appropriate ways: taking responsibility for a group task, listening to classmates, organising a school improvement activity, presenting evidence, reflecting on a failed attempt and revising a plan. Leadership should include humility and service, not only confidence or public speaking.

## 9. Assessment reform: change what the system rewards

A curriculum cannot reliably develop capabilities that assessment never recognises. Assessment is therefore one of the highest-leverage parts of reform—but it must be changed carefully.

### 9.1 A balanced assessment architecture

The proposed model has four layers:

| Layer | Purpose | Examples | Safeguards |
|---|---|---|---|
| Frequent formative checks | Identify misunderstanding and guide next teaching | Short retrieval questions, exit tickets, oral explanation, worked examples | Low stakes; use results to adjust instruction |
| Periodic common assessments | Check progress against shared standards | Moderated school tests, reading and numeracy checks, common subject tasks | Clear standards; compare learning growth, not just rank |
| Public certification examinations | Provide credible, comparable qualifications | SSC/HSC or successor examinations with knowledge, explanation and application | Transparent blueprint, security, accessible arrangements, independent moderation |
| Coursework and practical demonstrations | Assess extended application where appropriate | Lab practicals, technical demonstrations, projects, portfolios, oral defence | Individual contribution verified; public resources; common rubrics; manageable workload |

The balance should vary by subject and age. A practical subject needs a meaningful demonstration of practical competence. A literature examination should assess interpretation and evidence, not only generic project skills. A mathematics examination should include accurate procedures and problem-solving. A history examination should test knowledge of events as well as the ability to reason from sources.

### 9.2 How to improve examination papers

Every major examination should have a published assessment blueprint showing the intended balance of content, marks and cognitive demand. The blueprint should be based on subject experts, teachers, assessment specialists and curriculum standards. It should be piloted, reviewed for bias and updated transparently.

A useful item-coding rubric might distinguish:

- **Recall and recognition:** retrieve a fact, term or basic procedure;
- **Explanation:** describe why or how a concept works;
- **Routine application:** use a learned method in a familiar context;
- **Non-routine application:** select and adapt knowledge to a less familiar problem;
- **Analysis and evaluation:** compare evidence, test a claim or justify a decision; and
- **Extended or practical performance:** create, investigate, demonstrate or defend a solution where appropriate.

These categories are a proposed analytical tool, not a universal scale of value. Recall can be essential; a student cannot reason accurately about a subject without relevant knowledge. The concern is the distribution and alignment of marks, not the existence of recall questions.

### 9.3 Assessment must not become a paperwork machine

Continuous assessment can improve learning if teachers can interpret results and respond. It can also become a burden if every activity requires elaborate forms, uploads and reporting. Schools should use a small number of high-value indicators and simple record-keeping. Digital tools may help, but paper-based alternatives must remain available where connectivity is weak.

Teacher workload must be measured before scaling. If a new model requires more marking than teachers can realistically provide, the model should be simplified or resourced—not defended as innovative while implementation quietly deteriorates.

## 10. Teachers: the central condition of reform

Teachers are not merely the delivery mechanism for a policy. They are the professionals who interpret curriculum, notice misunderstanding, build trust and make daily decisions about what a child needs next. Any credible reform must invest in their knowledge, working conditions and professional judgement.

Four priorities follow.

**First, strengthen subject knowledge and pedagogy together.** A teacher needs to understand the subject and how students commonly misunderstand it. Training should include worked examples, modelling, questioning, explanation, feedback and adapting lessons to student responses.

**Second, replace one-off training with sustained coaching.** Workshops can introduce a method, but classroom practice changes through observation, feedback, collaboration and repeated use. School-based professional learning communities should examine student work and discuss what to teach next.

**Third, give teachers workable autonomy.** Teachers need clear national standards but also room to adapt examples, grouping and pacing to local conditions. Accountability should focus on whether students learn and whether professional duties are met, not on requiring identical classroom performance everywhere.

**Fourth, make the workload and resources real.** Teacher shortages, large classes, inadequate materials and administrative tasks can undermine good teaching. Reform plans should publish staffing assumptions, class-time requirements, training costs and resource allocations. Rural and remote teachers may need additional support and incentives.

Teachers should not be blamed for failing to deliver a system that has not equipped them to do so. At the same time, professional autonomy should come with responsibility to use evidence, treat students fairly and continue improving practice.

## 11. Equity, inclusion and student wellbeing

A future-ready system is not successful if it improves average scores while leaving disadvantaged children further behind. Equity must be measured directly, not treated as a paragraph at the end of a policy document.

The system should report outcomes, participation and progression by gender, disability, household socioeconomic status, geography, school type, language background and other locally relevant factors where data quality and privacy permit. It should distinguish a difference in outcomes from an explanation of that difference. Disaggregation can identify where support is needed; it does not automatically identify the cause.

Children with disabilities need accessible materials, reasonable accommodations, trained staff and assessment formats that measure the intended learning rather than an avoidable barrier. Rural students need access to qualified teachers, books, safe transport and learning resources. Students in poverty need public support so that participation does not depend on tutoring expenditure or the ability to purchase project materials. Students with different home languages may need explicit language support without having their home language treated as a deficit.

Wellbeing also matters to learning. Fear, humiliation and chronic overload can make it harder for students to ask questions, reveal misunderstanding and take intellectual risks. This does not mean removing effort or standards. It means creating a climate in which mistakes are information, feedback is respectful and help is available before a student falls irretrievably behind.

Schools should have clear, age-appropriate safeguards for bullying, harassment, violence, privacy and online safety. Student wellbeing data should be collected only when there is a clear purpose, confidentiality and a plan to respond. A wellbeing questionnaire without support or referral pathways is not an adequate policy.

## 12. Technology and AI: useful tools, not a substitute for teaching

Digital technology can widen access to explanations, reading materials, simulations, feedback and assistive tools. It can also distract students, expose personal information, amplify misinformation and increase inequality where access is uneven. UNESCO's Global Education Monitoring Report on technology in education emphasises that technology should be judged by whether it serves learning goals, not by the novelty of the device (UNESCO, 2023).

For Bangladesh, the practical question is not whether every classroom should become digital. It is which learning problems technology can solve at reasonable cost. Offline-capable resources may support schools with weak connectivity. Teacher-facing tools can help prepare examples or organise formative questions. Accessibility tools may support learners with disabilities. AI can provide additional practice, but its explanations may be inaccurate, its output may not match the curriculum, and students may become dependent on answers without developing understanding.

Any school use of AI should follow a simple hierarchy: the teacher and learning objective come first; the tool is selected second. Students should learn to verify claims, cite sources, protect personal information and recognise when an AI answer may be wrong. High-stakes grading, sensitive student profiling and automated decisions require stronger safeguards and human review.

Technology should not be used to disguise underinvestment in teachers, books or safe classrooms. A well-taught lesson with appropriate materials may be more valuable than an expensive platform that students cannot use consistently.

## 13. Financing and feasibility: ambition must match resources

A proposed education model is not serious unless it accounts for money, staffing, time and public administration. The system should publish transparent cost estimates for teacher training, classroom materials, accessible learning resources, assessment development, school infrastructure, remedial support and independent evaluation.

International benchmarks are useful as reference points, not automatic proof that a specific spending level will deliver results. UNESCO's Education 2030 framework has called on countries to allocate at least 4–6% of GDP and/or 15–20% of public expenditure to education. Bangladesh's own education spending data require careful reconciliation across budget heads, actual expenditure and fiscal years before making a precise current comparison. The reviewed evidence is not sufficient here to provide a fully audited, current education-spending series. That gap should be closed using official budget and expenditure tables before a final policy costing is published.

The core principle is to fund learning priorities before adding expensive requirements. For example, a pilot should not require every student to own a tablet if printed materials and shared devices can meet the objective. A project curriculum should not shift costs onto families. An assessment reform should budget for item development, moderation, accommodations and teacher time. A remedial programme should identify the students it aims to support and track whether they catch up.

Spending quality matters alongside spending volume. Budgets should show how much reaches classrooms, whether textbooks arrive on time, whether schools have qualified teachers and whether training changes practice. Procurement and grant processes should be transparent enough to make waste and unequal allocation visible.

## 14. Implementation roadmap: test, learn, adapt, scale

A national reform should not be launched everywhere at once merely to show urgency. The proposed roadmap uses stages, with explicit gates for continuation.

### Phase 1: Establish the baseline and agree the standards (first 6–12 months)

- Publish a concise set of foundational and later-stage learning standards.
- Review current textbooks, examinations and classroom expectations against those standards.
- Complete a representative analysis of SSC/HSC examination content.
- Extract official learning, attendance, teacher, school-facility and expenditure data.
- Consult students, teachers, parents, disability organisations, employers and researchers.
- Select pilot sites representing urban, rural, remote, different school types and socioeconomic contexts.
- Publish the evaluation design before implementation.

**Gate:** do not proceed to broad rollout until the standards, baseline measures, resource plan and assessment blueprint are public and internally coherent.

### Phase 2: Pilot with real teacher support (years 1–2)

Pilot a limited set of changes: foundational learning checks, targeted support, teacher coaching, balanced assessment tasks, student wellbeing safeguards and low-cost applied learning. The pilot should avoid adding every proposed feature at once; otherwise it will be difficult to know which components work.

Provide materials and training before teachers are held accountable for implementation. Record actual costs, teacher time, student participation and barriers. Include a comparison strategy where feasible, such as matched schools or a phased rollout, while recognising that random assignment may not always be practical or ethical.

**Gate:** continue only if implementation is sufficiently faithful to the design, student and teacher burden is manageable, and early data show plausible benefit without unacceptable equity harms.

### Phase 3: Independent evaluation and revision (years 2–3)

An independent team should examine learning growth, subgroup differences, attendance, progression, tutoring costs, teacher workload, student wellbeing and cost-effectiveness. It should report null or negative findings as openly as positive findings. The analysis plan should specify primary outcomes and subgroup comparisons in advance to reduce selective reporting.

The evaluation should distinguish whether a programme failed because its core idea was ineffective, because implementation was weak, or because the design was not suited to the context. These explanations imply different decisions.

**Gate:** revise, expand, pause or stop each component based on evidence. Do not scale a component merely because it is politically visible.

### Phase 4: Gradual expansion with continuous review (years 3–5 and beyond)

Scale components that show credible benefit and acceptable cost. Maintain independent sampling and public reporting. Preserve local adaptation within common standards. Review the curriculum on a predictable schedule, with transparent evidence and consultation, rather than through abrupt system-wide reversals.

A five-year horizon is a planning proposal, not a prediction. Implementation may take longer, especially where teacher supply, infrastructure or financing constraints are substantial.

## 15. Evaluation framework: how would we know whether reform works?

A reform should be judged by learning and opportunity, not by the number of workshops delivered, devices distributed or projects displayed.

### Table 3. Proposed evaluation dashboard

| Domain | Core measure | Disaggregation | Review frequency |
|---|---|---|---|
| Foundational learning | Reading comprehension, writing and numeracy against defined standards | Grade, gender, disability, geography, school type, socioeconomic context | Baseline and annual/periodic independent assessment |
| Learning depth | Explanation, application, reasoning and retention tasks | Subject, grade, subgroup | Sampled assessment each year |
| Progression | Attendance, grade transition, dropout and completion | Same key subgroups | Each school year |
| Equity | Gaps in access to teachers, materials, remedial support and outcomes | Rural/urban, income, disability and other relevant groups | Annual |
| Teaching conditions | Teacher attendance, vacancies, coaching, class size, workload and material availability | District and school type | Termly/annual |
| Assessment quality | Blueprint compliance, item demand, reliability, accommodations and marking consistency | Subject, examination board, candidate group | Each major examination cycle |
| Household burden | Tutoring and guidebook use, education costs and access to support | Income, geography, grade, school type | Periodic household survey |
| Wellbeing and safety | Student-reported belonging, stress, safety and help-seeking | Age-appropriate subgroups, with privacy protection | Periodic, not excessive |
| Technical/higher education transition | Further study, completion, employment quality and skills match | Programme, region, gender and socioeconomic context | Cohort follow-up |
| Value for money | Cost per learner supported and cost per unit of learning gain | Programme and context | Annual/independent review |

These measures require clear definitions and reliable collection. They should not all become high-stakes targets for individual teachers. If every indicator is tied to punishment, schools may learn to manipulate the numbers. A small set of primary outcomes should guide evaluation, with other indicators used diagnostically.

### Pre-specified decision rules

Before a pilot begins, the evaluation team should publish:

- the primary learning outcomes and minimum meaningful improvement;
- the comparison design and assumptions;
- how missing data and student movement will be handled;
- which subgroup harms would trigger review;
- the maximum acceptable teacher workload and household cost increase;
- what level of implementation is required to judge the intervention; and
- conditions under which a component will be revised, paused or stopped.

Exact thresholds should be set after the baseline and feasibility study, not invented in advance without reference to local variation, measurement reliability or cost. The absence of a universal threshold is not an excuse to evaluate informally; it is a reason to specify thresholds transparently before results are known.

## 16. Research priorities that could change the conclusion

This paper identifies several questions where stronger Bangladesh-specific evidence could materially change the recommendations.

**First, examination content.** What proportion of marks in representative SSC/HSC papers assesses recall, explanation, routine application, unfamiliar application, analysis and extended performance? How does that vary by subject and year? This requires item-level coding, double coding and transparent sampling.

**Second, tutoring and household cost.** How do tutoring access, spending, reasons for participation and perceived benefit vary by income, location, gender, disability and school type? Does tutoring compensate for classroom gaps, intensify competitive advantage, or do both under different conditions?

**Third, implementation.** How do curriculum and assessment policies actually translate into classroom practice? What do students do during lessons? How often do teachers use feedback to adapt instruction? What constraints do they identify? These questions require structured classroom observation, teacher interviews and student-work analysis.

**Fourth, longer-term outcomes.** Which school experiences predict later completion, technical competence, further study, employment quality, civic participation and wellbeing? National claims require longitudinal or carefully designed cohort data, not anecdotes about a few successful graduates.

**Fifth, cost and feasibility.** Which reforms improve learning at a reasonable cost under the staffing, class-size and infrastructure conditions of different Bangladeshi districts? A model that works in a well-resourced urban school may not transfer without adaptation.

These priorities should be treated as part of policy design, not as academic extras. A system that cannot measure whether its reforms work will repeatedly rely on political confidence, visible activity or examination pass rates as proxies for success.

## 17. Discussion: what does the evidence say about the central question?

The evidence supports a careful answer rather than a slogan.

**Is Bangladesh preparing students for the future?** It has articulated broader educational ambitions in its policy and curriculum frameworks. National assessments also show some improvement in selected foundational learning comparisons. These are meaningful starting points. They do not demonstrate that all students receive the learning experiences, subject mastery, reasoning practice and support that a future-ready system requires.

**Is the system mainly preparing students to pass examinations?** Widespread tutoring and guidebook use, strong family investment in supplementary preparation, and the recurring prominence of examination-linked progression are consistent with a system in which examination performance carries substantial weight. Policy documents themselves have recognised the risks of memorisation and examination orientation. But the evidence reviewed here is not sufficient to quantify how much current teaching is devoted to examination preparation or to prove that exam pressure alone explains weak foundational learning.

**What is the most defensible judgement?** Bangladesh faces a mismatch risk between broad educational ambitions and the incentives, resources and implementation conditions that shape daily learning. The answer is not to abolish examinations or to introduce fashionable projects everywhere. It is to align curriculum, classroom instruction, teacher support, assessment, financing and public accountability around a broader and clearly defined set of learning outcomes.

The distinction between knowledge and capability is especially important. A student cannot think critically about a topic they do not understand. A student who memorises without comprehension may fail to use knowledge. The system must therefore develop both: secure knowledge and increasingly independent application. This is not a compromise between rigour and creativity; it is a more complete definition of rigour.

The international evidence also cautions against simple solutions. High-performing systems still face inequalities and staffing problems. Learning techniques are most useful when integrated into good teaching rather than marketed as isolated tricks. Technology can expand access but cannot replace skilled teachers or public provision. Reform must be adapted to the real conditions of Bangladeshi classrooms and revised when evidence contradicts the design.

## 18. Conclusion: from examination success to learning that lasts

Bangladesh's students deserve an education that prepares them not only to answer a paper, but to understand the world the paper represents. They need to read with comprehension, use mathematics, know their subjects, reason from evidence, communicate, collaborate, make ethical decisions, learn new skills and contribute to their communities. They also need fair examinations and recognised qualifications. These goals are compatible when the system is designed coherently.

The available evidence points to a foundational learning challenge, widespread reliance on private tutoring and guidebooks in the studied populations, substantial household costs and a policy environment in which implementation and continuity matter greatly. It does not justify blaming a single group or claiming that one reform will solve the problem. Nor does it support a complete verdict on all secondary, vocational and higher education outcomes, where this review's evidence remains thinner.

The practical direction is clear even while the exact design must be tested: protect foundational learning; keep academic knowledge central; reward explanation and application as well as recall; support teachers as professionals; reduce the extent to which family income determines access to supplementary learning; make technical and academic pathways respected and permeable; use technology only where it improves learning; and evaluate reforms independently before scaling them.

Preparing the next generation of Bangladeshi leaders means giving every child a fair chance to become capable, thoughtful and responsible—not promising that every student will become famous, wealthy or powerful. Leadership grows from knowledge, judgement, practice, opportunity and trust. The education system should cultivate these conditions from childhood and keep them open throughout life.

A credible reform will not be judged by how modern its language sounds. It will be judged by whether children learn more, whether the poorest and most excluded children benefit, whether teachers can deliver the promised education, whether families face a fairer burden, and whether graduates can use what they know. The test of a future-ready system is not whether it has abandoned examinations. It is whether examinations serve learning, rather than learning being reduced to examinations.
