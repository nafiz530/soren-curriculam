# Step 4 — Research Methodology & Evidence Standards

**Author:** Md. Nafiz  
**Phase:** 1 — Research Foundation  
**Status:** Draft — review pending  
**Official research title:** *Is our education system actually preparing students for the future, or is it mainly preparing students to pass examinations?*

## 1. Why the method must be explicit

This research could reach almost any conclusion if I searched only for sources that agreed with my starting assumptions, treated policy statements as proof of success, or selected international examples because they looked attractive. The subject is too consequential for that approach.

A method does not guarantee a correct conclusion. It makes the route to a conclusion more visible: what I asked, where I searched, what I selected, how I judged it, which competing explanations I considered, and why I believe the evidence supports a particular statement.

The project is a structured, transparent, critical evidence synthesis. At this stage, it is **not presented as a completed systematic review**. The project will use systematic-review practices where appropriate—clear questions, documented searches, explicit selection criteria, source appraisal, and transparent synthesis—without claiming exhaustive coverage that has not been demonstrated.

PRISMA 2020 is useful as a model for transparent reporting of systematic reviews. It is a reporting guideline, not a universal tool for grading source quality, and it does not turn an informal search into a systematic review. The What Works Clearinghouse (WWC) handbook offers more education-specific procedures for evaluating intervention studies, but its standards and intended context must be understood rather than applied mechanically to every philosophical, historical, or Bangladesh-specific question. [PRISMA 2020](https://www.bmj.com/content/372/bmj.n71) · [WWC Procedures and Standards Handbook, Version 5.0](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Handbooks).

## 2. Match the method to the question

No single method answers every question in education.

A philosophical question about what education ought to serve needs explicit concepts, arguments, values, and counterarguments. A question about what Bangladesh's policy formally says needs the primary policy document and careful version control. A claim that a reform caused improved learning requires stronger causal reasoning than a report showing that results improved after the reform. A question about how students experience an arrangement may need qualitative evidence that a test score cannot provide.

I will therefore classify each major question before deciding what evidence is suitable. The method table in the step plan is a starting protocol. It prevents me from using a convenient source to answer a different question from the one it can actually support.

## 3. Source credibility is claim-relative

I will not use “official,” “peer-reviewed,” “international,” or “popular” as a shortcut for “true.” Each label tells me something about a source's origin or process, but none settles whether a specific claim is supported.

An official document is often the strongest source for the exact policy an institution issued. It may be weak evidence for whether that policy improved learning. A study can use rigorous methods and still be irrelevant to a different country or population. A theoretical paper can clarify a mechanism without establishing how often it occurs. A learner's account can illuminate lived experience without estimating how common that experience is.

I will ask what each source can establish, how it establishes it, and where the inference stops. Sources should be evaluated against the question and claim, not ranked once and used indiscriminately.

## 4. Search transparently and seek disconfirmation

For important topics, I will record search dates, locations, exact search terms, relevant limits, inclusion criteria, selected sources, and meaningful exclusions. I will search in English and Bangla when local evidence or terminology makes that necessary. For Bangladesh policy history, I will prioritize dated primary documents and distinguish announcements from formal adoption and implementation.

I will actively search for evidence that challenges my current interpretation. That includes null findings, adverse outcomes, alternative explanations, methodological critiques, and credible arguments for practices I may initially question. I will not infer that an examination or a traditional practice is harmful merely because it is old, nor that a reform is beneficial merely because it is new.

Search coverage will remain limited by time, access, language, indexing, and the availability of documents. Unless the search protocol and coverage justify it, I will say “the sources located in this search” rather than “all available evidence.”

## 5. Separate evidence from interpretation and choice

The project uses the following labels:

| Label | Meaning | What it must not be confused with |
|---|---|---|
| Established evidence | A claim supported by sufficiently reliable and relevant evidence within a stated scope | A universal truth beyond the evidence |
| Finding | A result reported by a source or transparently derived from data | My explanation of what the result means |
| Interpretation | My reasoned account of what a finding may imply | The finding itself |
| Normative argument | A reasoned claim about what should matter or what ought to be done | An empirical claim that something works |
| Research-backed recommendation | A proposal supported by evidence and an explicit rationale, with limitations stated | A guaranteed outcome |
| Design choice | A decision selected for the proposed system, including values and constraints | A scientific discovery |
| Hypothesis | A testable proposition not yet adequately established | A confirmed finding |
| Speculation | A possibility with insufficient current support for a firmer label | A conclusion fit to guide major policy alone |

A recommendation may be reasonable even when evidence is incomplete, but its uncertainty and value assumptions must be explicit. Equally, a finding may be strong while leaving the normative question unresolved. For example, evidence about how an assessment affects behaviour can inform whether it is desirable; it cannot alone decide what educational purposes should take priority.

## 6. Appraise empirical research carefully

For quantitative research, I will examine the sample, population, measures, missing data, comparison conditions, uncertainty, effect size, analytic decisions, and possible confounders. I will distinguish statistical significance from practical importance and avoid broad claims from narrow samples.

For causal claims, I will ask what would likely have happened without the intervention, how comparisons were made, what else changed, and whether alternative explanations remain. The UK government's 2026 *Magenta Book* emphasizes evaluation suited to the question and distinguishes process, impact, and value-for-money evaluation. Its guidance on theory-based evaluation also stresses testing causal pathways, triangulating evidence, considering alternative causes, and allowing external scrutiny. These are useful methodological principles, not a reason to assume a UK policy method transfers unchanged to Bangladesh. [Magenta Book](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/the-magenta-book) · [Annex A: Analytical methods](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/the-magenta-book/magenta-book-annex-a-analytical-methods-for-use-within-an-evaluation-html).

For qualitative research, I will examine how participants and settings were selected, how information was collected, how interpretations were developed, whether contradictory cases were considered, and how the researchers' position may shape the analysis. Qualitative evidence can illuminate experience and mechanism; it should not be forced to answer a prevalence question it was not designed to answer.

For mixed-method research, I will ask whether the methods genuinely complement one another. Agreement among several sources is less persuasive when they all repeat the same original claim. Triangulation is a way to challenge and enrich an interpretation, not a simple vote-count.

## 7. Synthesize disagreement instead of hiding it

When credible sources disagree, I will not select whichever result supports the desired conclusion. I will identify whether the disagreement concerns definitions, samples, contexts, methods, outcomes, time horizons, or interpretation. I will compare the evidence, investigate plausible reasons for differences, and state what remains unresolved.

The number of studies is not enough. Many small or methodologically weak studies do not automatically outweigh a better-designed study, while a single strong study may still be insufficient for a broad system-wide claim. The strength of a conclusion depends on the total relevant evidence, its quality and independence, and the scope of the claim.

“Mixed evidence,” “context-dependent,” and “not yet known” are legitimate conclusions when they accurately describe the evidence. Uncertainty should lead to a more precise statement or a further research question, not an invented certainty.

## 8. Ethics and integrity

This project currently relies mainly on existing documents and published evidence. If I later collect original information from students, teachers, families, or other people, I must consider informed and voluntary participation, privacy, data minimization, risk of harm, secure handling, and relevant school, guardian, legal, and ethics requirements. Research involving minors needs special protection and must not begin casually or without appropriate permissions and safeguards.

The American Educational Research Association's Code of Ethics foregrounds competence, integrity, professional responsibility, respect for people's rights and dignity, and social responsibility. I will use it as a professional reference, while recognizing that a code developed for researchers does not replace applicable local law or institutional requirements. [AERA Professional Ethics](https://www.aera.net/about-aera/aera-rules-policies/professional-ethics).

I will cite ideas and data accurately, distinguish quotation from paraphrase, represent sources fairly, note important corrections, disclose relevant limitations, and not invent findings or citations. The aim is not to claim that personal bias can be eliminated. It is to make assumptions visible and open to challenge.

## 9. From research to a defensible decision

A major design decision should be traceable through the evidence and reasoning that support it. Where appropriate, I will document:

**Question → Search and selection → Source appraisal → Finding → Interpretation → Counterevidence → Uncertainty → Value or design principle → Proposed decision → Expected outcome → Failure mode**

Not every philosophical claim can be reduced to a causal chain, and not every educational value can be measured by a single indicator. The traceability structure should fit the type of question while making the reasoning inspectable.

A proposed system should not be called “evidence-based” simply because its features sound sensible or resemble a successful example. It must explain which evidence supports which part, what assumptions connect evidence to the proposal, who may benefit or lose, and what future evaluation could prove the proposal wrong.

## 10. Provisional commitments

I will use these rules throughout the research unless later evidence or a documented methodological reason justifies revising them:

1. Match the evidence and method to the exact claim being made.
2. Prefer direct and independently verifiable evidence for important factual claims, without treating source type as a substitute for appraisal.
3. Seek credible counterevidence and alternative explanations deliberately.
4. Separate policy intent, formal adoption, implementation, outcomes, and causal claims.
5. State uncertainty and research limitations rather than filling gaps with confident assumptions.
6. Make major recommendations traceable to evidence, interpretation, values, trade-offs, and possible failure modes.
7. Do not claim systematic-review completeness unless the actual search and screening process warrants it.
8. Treat ethics, privacy, dignity, and participant protection as essential if the project later involves people.

These commitments make the research more accountable; they do not guarantee neutrality, exhaustiveness, or correctness.

## 11. Open questions for later research

1. Which research databases and Bangla-language repositories should be prioritized for each later topic?
2. What practical threshold will distinguish a source that is merely relevant from one strong enough to support a major recommendation?
3. When should a topic receive a formal systematic review rather than a structured evidence synthesis?
4. How should the project document searches that cannot be reproduced exactly because a platform changes or access is limited?
5. What local ethical and institutional approvals would be required before gathering original data from Bangladeshi students or teachers?
6. How should conflicting evidence be summarized when studies use incompatible measures?
7. Which independent reviewers or subject experts could later challenge high-impact conclusions?

These questions do not all need answers before this draft can be reviewed. They should be resolved when they materially affect the next research task or a major conclusion.
