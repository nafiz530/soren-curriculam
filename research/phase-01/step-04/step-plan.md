# Step 4 — Research Methodology & Evidence Standards

**Author:** Md. Nafiz  
**Phase:** 1 — Research Foundation  
**Status:** Approved as the working protocol (2026-10-09)  
**Official research title:** *Is our education system actually preparing students for the future, or is it mainly preparing students to pass examinations?*

## Purpose

Steps 1–3 established the research question, clarified the meaning of education, and mapped the purposes education may serve. Before investigating learning science, existing systems, and proposed reforms, I need a transparent method for deciding what to trust, how to compare unlike kinds of evidence, and how to keep findings separate from interpretations and design choices.

This step establishes the method for the project. It does not claim that the project is already a systematic review, and it does not predetermine which education model the evidence will support.

## Main question

> How can I conduct and document a transparent, critical, context-sensitive investigation of education that distinguishes what sources establish from what I infer, value, recommend, or hypothesize?

## Questions the protocol must answer during later research

1. Which kinds of questions will the project ask, and what evidence is suitable for each?
2. How will sources be found, recorded, screened, and revisited?
3. How will I appraise authority, methods, relevance, recency, independence, context, and limitations?
4. How should primary documents, peer-reviewed studies, official statistics, policy reports, theoretical works, journalism, and lived experience be used differently?
5. How will I compare quantitative, qualitative, mixed-method, historical, philosophical, and policy evidence without forcing them into one ranking?
6. How will I distinguish association from causation and avoid attributing outcomes to a reform without adequate evidence?
7. How will I synthesize conflicting results, negative findings, uncertainty, and plausible alternative explanations?
8. How will I prevent selective citation, confirmation bias, political preference, institutional prestige, and attractive narratives from deciding the conclusion in advance?
9. How should I record changes to search strategies, inclusion criteria, interpretations, and provisional decisions?
10. What level of evidence is needed for a descriptive claim, a causal claim, a normative argument, or a proposed design decision?
11. What ethical requirements apply if the project later collects information from students, teachers, families, or other people, especially minors?
12. What can a student-led desk-research project reasonably claim, and what must remain uncertain or deferred?

These are questions the protocol must answer during application. They are not all separate approval blockers: the approved manuscript and operational protocol specify how they will be handled, while topic-specific details remain to be completed when each research question is investigated.

## Proposed research design

Use a **structured, transparent, critical evidence synthesis** combining:
- conceptual and philosophical analysis for definitions, values, and arguments;
- policy and historical document analysis for stated aims, decisions, and institutional changes;
- quantitative and qualitative empirical research for outcomes, experiences, mechanisms, and inequalities;
- comparative analysis for similarities, differences, and contextual transferability;
- evaluation guidance for judging intervention claims and implementation.

This is the approved working design, not a claim that every search has been exhaustive. I will call a review “systematic” only if its actual procedures justify that label. PRISMA 2020 is a reporting guideline for systematic reviews, not a universal quality score and not a substitute for good appraisal.

## Question-to-method matching

| Question type | Examples in this project | Suitable evidence and methods | Main caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Conceptual | What counts as education or learning? | Philosophical scholarship, conceptual analysis, operational definitions | Definitions may be contested; do not hide normative choices inside terminology. |
| Normative | What should education serve? | Ethical and political philosophy, rights instruments, reasoned public arguments, affected perspectives | Empirical evidence informs consequences but cannot alone settle value questions. |
| Descriptive | What policy or curriculum was adopted? | Primary laws, policies, official notices, archived versions, implementation records | A policy's stated aim does not prove implementation or impact. |
| Empirical association | Which outcomes are associated with a practice? | Observational studies, datasets, transparent descriptive analyses | Association is not proof of causation. |
| Causal | Did a reform cause an outcome? | Randomized or credible quasi-experimental designs where feasible; theory-based and mixed-method evaluation | Confounding, selection, implementation differences, and alternative explanations may remain. |
| Mechanism and experience | How and why does a practice work for some learners? | Qualitative studies, interviews, observation, process evaluation, mixed methods | Context matters; a small study can offer depth without establishing prevalence. |
| Historical | How did a system or practice develop? | Original records, dated policy versions, archival scholarship, historiography | Later accounts can simplify or politicize the past; corroborate important claims. |
| Comparative | What can Bangladesh learn from another system? | Comparable indicators, contextual case studies, comparative research | Similar labels may hide different institutions, resources, populations, or aims. |
| Feasibility and implementation | Could a proposed approach work in practice? | Cost/resource data, implementation studies, stakeholder experience, pilots, risk analysis | Plausibility is not demonstrated effectiveness; local testing may be required. |

## Source appraisal framework

Do not use a single rigid hierarchy for every question. Appraise a source against the claim it is being used to support.

1. **Identity and provenance:** Who produced it, when, for what purpose, and in which institutional context?
2. **Question fit:** Does the source answer this question, or is it being stretched beyond its scope?
3. **Method transparency:** Are data, sample, measures, definitions, selection criteria, and analytical steps visible enough to assess?
4. **Credibility of evidence:** Are the claims supported by the presented material? Are uncertainty and limitations acknowledged?
5. **Independence and interests:** Who funded or commissioned the work? Are conflicts of interest or advocacy aims disclosed?
6. **Context and transferability:** Which population, country, period, school type, policy, and implementation conditions does it represent?
7. **Recency and historical value:** Is currency important for this claim, or is the older source essential to understanding origins or theory?
8. **Corroboration and disagreement:** Do independent sources converge? What credible counterevidence or rival explanation exists?
9. **Integrity:** Can the cited passage, data, document version, or result be checked? Are corrections or retractions relevant?
10. **Limits of inference:** What does the source not establish?

An official source is especially useful for documenting what an institution formally said or published; official status does not automatically validate its causal claims. A peer-reviewed article has passed a review process, but that alone does not make every method or conclusion sound. A first-person account can reveal experience and meaning but cannot, by itself, establish how common that experience is. These are different uses, not a simple contest over which source type is always best.

## Search and screening protocol

For each substantial research question, record:
- the question and why it matters;
- search date and search location/database/site;
- exact search terms or queries, including relevant Bangla and English terms;
- date range and other limits, if any;
- inclusion and exclusion criteria;
- sources found and sources selected for close reading;
- reasons for excluding potentially relevant items when exclusion could affect the conclusion;
- citation details, stable URL or identifier, access date where appropriate, and archived file path if saved;
- important source gaps and follow-up searches.

Search beyond sources that support the initial interpretation. Use reference lists and citation trails to locate original studies and credible counterarguments. For politically contested or rapidly changing issues, date every status claim and seek primary records where possible. Search engines and databases have coverage limits; an undocumented search should not be described as exhaustive.

## Evidence, inference, and decision labels

Every material claim or conclusion should be classifiable as one of the following:

- **Established evidence:** meets the claim-relative threshold in the Step 4 manuscript and has scope/counterevidence recorded.
- **Finding:** a result reported by a source or transparently derived from data.
- **Interpretation:** my explanation of what a finding may mean.
- **Normative argument:** a reasoned claim about what should be valued, permitted, or prioritized.
- **Research-backed recommendation:** a recommendation supported by evidence and explicit rationale, with uncertainty and trade-offs.
- **Design choice:** a decision selected for the proposed system, including value judgement or practical constraint.
- **Expert judgment:** attributed, context-specific judgement from relevant practical or scholarly experience, without implying representative evidence.
- **Hypothesis:** a proposition that could be tested but has not yet been adequately established.
- **Speculation:** a possibility with insufficient support for a firmer claim.

Do not upgrade a hypothesis into a finding through repetition. Do not call a design choice “evidence-based” unless the evidence-to-decision reasoning is made visible.

## Appraising empirical claims

For quantitative studies, examine sample and sampling, measurement validity, missing data, comparison groups, effect size and uncertainty, analytical choices, relevant confounders, and whether the population matches the claim. Statistical significance alone does not establish educational importance.

For causal claims, ask what would likely have happened without the intervention, how comparison conditions were created, whether groups differed before treatment, whether implementation varied, and which alternative explanations remain. A randomized trial can be powerful for some questions but does not automatically answer questions about long-term system-wide change, values, mechanisms, or transfer to Bangladesh.

For qualitative work, examine participant selection, setting, researcher position, data collection, analytical transparency, contradictory cases, and whether interpretations are grounded in the material. Do not dismiss qualitative evidence simply because it does not estimate population prevalence.

For mixed-method and theory-based work, examine whether the methods answer complementary questions, whether the proposed mechanism is actually tested, and whether apparently convergent evidence is genuinely independent. Triangulation improves scrutiny; it does not make weak sources strong by counting them together.

## Synthesis and disagreement

Do not count studies as votes. Compare their methods, populations, definitions, settings, outcomes, and limitations. A large number of weak or dependent sources does not necessarily outweigh one strong, directly relevant source. Equally, a single study should not settle a broad policy question when its scope is narrow.

When sources disagree:
1. state exactly what the disagreement concerns;
2. check whether the studies measure the same construct or outcome;
3. compare their populations, periods, methods, and implementation conditions;
4. examine uncertainty, null results, adverse outcomes, and publication or reporting bias;
5. test alternative explanations;
6. state what is supported, what is disputed, and what further evidence would change the conclusion.

A conclusion may be “mixed,” “context-dependent,” or “not yet known.” Those are legitimate findings when justified, not failures to decide.

## Bangladesh context and transferability

Bangladesh is the primary contextual case. International findings may identify mechanisms or useful comparisons, but recommendations must consider the local education structure, language, resources, geography, social conditions, governance, assessment incentives, and implementation capacity. I will not assume that an intervention transfers because it worked elsewhere or because an international organization recommends it.

Where evidence is missing, distinguish “no evidence located in this search” from “evidence shows no effect.” Record local evidence gaps as research priorities rather than filling them with confident assumptions.

## Research ethics and integrity

This phase primarily uses existing documents and published evidence. If I later collect original information from people, I must first consider consent, privacy, data minimization, risk of harm, voluntary participation, secure handling, and any school, guardian, ethics, or legal requirements. Research involving minors requires special care and must not be undertaken casually or without appropriate permission and safeguards.

Represent sources fairly, cite borrowed ideas, distinguish quotation from paraphrase, record corrections, disclose important limitations, and avoid fabricating or selectively omitting inconvenient evidence. Protect participants and communities from unnecessary identification or harm.

## Traceability standard

Major proposed design decisions should, where relevant, be traceable through:

**Question → Search and selection → Source appraisal → Finding → Interpretation → Counterevidence → Uncertainty → Value or design principle → Proposed decision → Expected outcome → Failure mode**

Not every philosophical or conceptual claim follows a causal chain, and not every decision can be reduced to an effect estimate. The trace should fit the question and make the reasoning inspectable.

## Provisional commitments for this project

1. The method must be transparent enough that another reader can understand how important sources and conclusions were reached.
2. Evidence standards must fit the claim type; no single source hierarchy can replace appraisal.
3. Credible counterarguments and null or adverse findings must be actively sought.
4. Claims about Bangladesh's policies and reforms must distinguish formal decisions, public announcements, implementation, and outcomes.
5. No system design should be treated as established merely because it is coherent, attractive, or aligned with declared aims.
6. Research scope, limitations, and unresolved questions must be stated honestly.

These commitments are open to review and refinement. They are not a claim that bias can be eliminated; the goal is to make it visible, challengeable, and less likely to control the conclusion.

## Required operational artifacts

Before title-level synthesis, define and record observable working constructs for both “future preparation” and “examination preparation,” then assess each source's construct match. Use the repository's evidence-table and search-log templates.

For every high-impact conclusion, log the strongest opposing case, supporting evidence, assumptions, missing evidence, and what would change the conclusion. Record funding/commissioning, language, publication risks, and trace-to-origin checks for load-bearing statistics. Full traceability is mandatory for title-level synthesis, major causal/comparative claims, national policy-status claims, major statistics, and major recommendations; background claims receive proportionate documentation.

Use the established-evidence threshold: two independent lines of evidence or one high-quality closely matched study, plus a documented search for credible counterevidence. Major recommendations require the complete traceability chain. Normative disagreements must state value premises, counterarguments, the researcher's weights, and the Step 3 evaluation criteria.
