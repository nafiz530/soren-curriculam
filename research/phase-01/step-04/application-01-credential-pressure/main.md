# Method Application 01 — Why Examination Pressure Persists in Bangladesh

**Author:** Md. Nafiz  
**Status:** DRAFT — for discussion and review; no substantive conclusion approved  
**Research question:** Why does examination pressure persist in Bangladesh, and how much is explained by learning, certification, selection, credential competition, signaling, and wider institutional incentives?

## 1. What this pilot can and cannot answer

I am testing the Step 4 method on a narrow, consequential case. The evidence located so far supports investigation of examination-oriented teaching and unequal access to supplementary tutoring. It does **not** establish that Bangladesh's education system as a whole mainly prepares students to pass examinations, nor does it show that examinations have no valid learning or certification function.

A key limitation is construct mismatch. Most Bangladesh-specific sources located so far examine SSC English teaching or private English tutoring. They do not measure the full range of future preparation—durable knowledge, transfer, judgement, agency, civic participation, and later learning/work outcomes. They can illuminate mechanisms behind exam pressure but cannot by themselves settle the title-level comparison.

## 2. Four functions that must not be collapsed

1. **Learning and assessment:** an examination may provide information about what a learner can do, depending on validity, coverage, conditions, and how results are interpreted.
2. **Certification:** a result may attest that a learner has reached a stated standard or completed a stage.
3. **Selection and ranking:** results may allocate scarce seats, scholarships, or other opportunities. A test used for ranking can create pressure beyond its value as a learning measure.
4. **Signaling:** where institutions or employers cannot directly observe capability, a credential may convey information. Spence's model explains a possible mechanism; it does not prove that every qualification is informative or that the mechanism dominates in Bangladesh.

Dore's credential-competition argument asks whether, when qualifications are used to screen candidates for scarce opportunities, people compete for credentials even when the extra credential does not correspond to an equivalent increase in relevant capability. Collins's credential-society thesis examines how credential requirements can regulate occupational access and status; Hirsch's positional-goods framework explains why relative advantage can remain scarce even as educational participation expands. These are related but distinct explanations: signaling concerns information under uncertainty; credentialism concerns how qualifications regulate access and stratification; positional competition concerns rewards that depend on relative rank. All three are hypotheses to test locally, not conclusions to import unchanged from comparative work.

## 3. Provisional findings from the first evidence pass

### Finding A — Examination-oriented practice is documented in sampled SSC English settings

Rahman et al. (2021; SRC-035) report a qualitative study across 12 secondary schools using teacher interviews, student focus groups, and classroom observations. They report negative washback on English teaching and learning and identify curriculum–test mismatch, teacher understanding, attitudes, and pressure from schools and parents as contributing factors.

**Interpretation:** this is directly relevant evidence that examination format and surrounding pressure can shape teaching practice in the settings studied. Because the sample was selected by convenience and the focus is SSC English, it does not estimate national prevalence or prove that the same pattern occurs in all subjects and schools.

Ali and Hamid (2020; SRC-036) interviewed 12 secondary teachers and argue that negative washback is embedded in broader social, economic, political, and accountability conditions, rather than being caused by test design alone.

Al Amin and Greenwood (2018; SRC-040) provide a broader mixed-methods investigation of Bangladesh secondary English education. Their study began with a survey of 216 English teachers and included qualitative interviews with students, teachers, teacher trainers, principals, parents, and other professionals, alongside observation and document analysis. The authors report pressure from parents, students, and school leaders to secure high grades, and accounts of teachers omitting content they considered less likely to be tested. They interpret the exam system as sustained by social expectations, school/teacher reputation, coaching businesses, and the high stakes attached to progression and future opportunities.

**Interpretation:** the two studies are compatible with a multi-mechanism explanation. They do not establish the relative causal weight of each mechanism, and their qualitative designs should not be presented as national causal estimates.

### Finding B — Exam pressure and credential incentives are plausible, distinct mechanisms

Spence (1973; SRC-014) models signaling under labor-market information asymmetry. Dore's The Diploma Disease (SRC-012) examines qualification competition and the use of educational achievement as a screening device. Collins (SRC-041) examines credentialism and occupational stratification; Hirsch (SRC-042) explains positional competition where relative access to scarce opportunities matters. These sources help formulate different mechanisms and predictions.

**Interpretation:** testable questions include whether employers and institutions rely on credentials because they predict relevant capability, because they need a practical screening device, or both; whether competition for scarce opportunities increases the value of scores independently of learning; and which decisions actually depend on SSC/HSC results.

**Evidence status:** theoretical mechanism, not Bangladesh-specific causal evidence. Direct local evidence about admission rules, hiring practices, returns to qualifications, and the relationship between credentials and competence is still needed.

### Finding C — Supplementary tutoring is connected to both support and inequality, but evidence is heterogeneous

Mahmud (2021; SRC-037) reports that a 2014–2015 survey found English tutoring among 84.7% of urban and 60.7% of rural secondary students. These figures are historical, subject-specific, and need full-text checks of sampling and weighting before use as load-bearing statistics. They must not be described as current national tutoring rates across all subjects.

Islam, Hoque, and Hoque (2021; SRC-038) use interviews with 11 higher-secondary students to explore perceived inequality, financial access, and social expectations around English tutoring.

The ADB's 2024 randomized trial report (SRC-039) finds positive math and Bangla score effects from an intervention combining tablets and private tutoring for out-of-school children during the COVID-19 pandemic. This is counterevidence against the blanket claim that tutoring is inherently harmful. However, the combined intervention and distinct population mean the result does not identify the separate effect of tutoring or establish the net effect of routine fee-based tutoring among mainstream SSC/HSC students.

**Interpretation:** tutoring should be analyzed by purpose, quality, cost, access, and context. It can provide additional instruction; it can also become a costly requirement for navigating exams and widen inequalities when access depends on family resources. These propositions require further direct testing rather than a single universal verdict.

## 4. Why the pressure may persist: a provisional mechanism map

The following are hypotheses to test, not a completed causal explanation:

- **High-stakes consequences:** if results influence progression or access to limited opportunities, families and schools have incentives to prioritize score improvement.
- **Information and screening:** institutions may rely on standardized results because they are visible and comparable, even if they capture only part of capability.
- **Accountability and reputation:** schools and teachers may be judged by results, reinforcing focus on tested material.
- **Family uncertainty and competition:** families may purchase tutoring to reduce perceived risk or avoid falling behind peers.
- **Unequal support:** households with more resources may buy additional preparation, creating unequal access to exam strategies or instruction.
- **Curriculum–assessment mismatch:** when intended curriculum and actual test format diverge, teaching may follow the test rather than the broader curriculum.

These mechanisms can reinforce each other. The current evidence does not rank them or show which is decisive in Bangladesh.

## 5. The strongest counterarguments

- Examinations can provide a common certification standard and may be more transparent than unstructured discretionary selection in some settings.
- Some exam-focused practice may reinforce useful knowledge; negative washback is not automatic.
- Tutoring can provide real instructional support, as the ADB intervention evidence cautions.
- The problem may lie in implementation, resource inequality, curriculum–test mismatch, excessive stakes, or selection scarcity—not in assessment itself.
- Replacing exams without a credible alternative could increase subjectivity, favoritism, cost, or inconsistency.

A fair investigation must compare these claims against the evidence rather than treating them as rhetorical objections.

## 6. What I cannot conclude yet

I cannot currently conclude that examination pressure is the single or main cause of weak learning outcomes in Bangladesh; that all SSC/HSC subjects are taught to the test to the same degree; that private tutoring generally improves or worsens learning outcomes; that credentials primarily signal ability rather than certify learning or enable selection; that reducing exam stakes alone would reduce inequality or improve future readiness; or that the figures reported for 2014–2015 describe current tutoring prevalence.

## 7. Evidence needed next

1. Read the full methods and results of SRC-035–039 and record precise page/section references, sample details, measures, uncertainty, and funding/commissioning.
2. Search Bangla and English for SSC/HSC washback beyond English and for current tutoring patterns.
3. Verify official progression, admission, and selection rules from primary Bangladesh records.
4. Investigate employer use of SSC/HSC and later qualifications, and whether credentials predict task-relevant competence.
5. Read Hirsch and Collins in depth, compare their distinct mechanisms with Dore and Spence, and test predictions against Bangladesh-specific evidence.
6. Verify NEP 2010 wording and the 2012 curriculum rollout against primary documents; do not substitute secondary commentary.
7. Trace historical tutoring percentages to original datasets and do not rely on abstract-level figures as final load-bearing evidence.
8. Define how future-preparation outcomes could be measured before comparing them with exam-preparation indicators.

## 8. Provisional bottom line

The first pass gives a defensible reason to investigate examination pressure as a system of interacting incentives, not merely a test-design issue. Bangladesh-specific qualitative studies document examination-oriented practice in sampled secondary English settings; tutoring studies raise questions about both support and unequal access; economic theory supplies mechanisms for why credentials may become valuable in competition.

The evidence is not yet sufficient for a national conclusion about the purpose or effects of Bangladesh's whole education system. This remains a provisional synthesis and must pass full-text appraisal, a Bangla-language search, primary-policy verification, and an explicit devil's-advocate review before any high-impact conclusion is accepted.
