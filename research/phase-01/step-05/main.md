# Step 5 — How Humans Learn

**Author:** Md. Nafiz  
**Status:** DRAFT — not approved  
**Research question:** What does reliable evidence tell us about how people learn, remember, understand, transfer knowledge, develop skills, and regulate their own learning?

## 1. Why this step matters

Before deciding what students should learn, how schools should teach, or how achievement should be assessed, I need a defensible account of learning itself. Popular educational claims often jump from a plausible idea to a universal prescription. The evidence does not justify that shortcut.

Learning is not one outcome. A student may remember a fact for a test, understand its explanation, apply a principle to a new problem, perform a procedure fluently, or independently recognize when the knowledge is useful. These outcomes overlap, but they are not interchangeable. The National Academies' *How People Learn II* describes learning as shaped by interactions among cognition, prior knowledge, motivation, development, language, culture, relationships, and the learning environment. It also emphasizes disciplinary knowledge, metacognition, and the conditions under which knowledge can be transferred. [Source: National Academies, 2018](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/24783/chapter/9).

## 2. Distinguish performance from learning

A good score immediately after instruction does not automatically demonstrate durable retention or transfer. Likewise, a difficult practice session does not necessarily mean learning has failed. I will therefore keep at least these outcomes separate:

- **Acquisition:** initial development of knowledge or skill.
- **Immediate performance:** what a learner can do now, under the current conditions.
- **Retention:** what remains available after a delay.
- **Understanding:** ability to explain relationships, principles, or reasons rather than only reproduce a response.
- **Transfer:** ability to use knowledge in a meaningfully different context or problem.
- **Fluency and skill:** reliable performance with appropriate accuracy, efficiency, and judgement.
- **Self-regulation:** ability to plan, monitor, and adapt one's learning.

Every claim about a strategy should specify which outcome was measured, how it was measured, and when it was measured. A gain in one outcome must not be silently generalized to all the others.

## 3. Prior knowledge, memory, and cognitive constraints

New learning is not built on an empty mind. Learners interpret new information through existing knowledge, experiences, language, beliefs, and misconceptions. Organized knowledge can help learners notice patterns and reason more effectively; gaps or misconceptions can also obstruct new learning. Instruction therefore needs to establish what learners know and support connections between familiar and new ideas.

Working memory has limits, but the practical implication is not that all complex tasks should be removed. It is that unnecessary complexity, confusing presentation, and unsupported task demands can compete with the mental resources required for learning. The appropriate balance depends on prior knowledge, task complexity, and the support available. Worked examples, guided practice, and gradual movement toward independent performance are hypotheses to evaluate in the relevant domain—not rules to apply identically everywhere.

## 4. Retrieval and spacing: promising evidence, not magic formulas

Dunlosky and colleagues' 2013 review rated practice testing and distributed practice as high-utility techniques across the evidence they reviewed, while other strategies had more conditional or limited support. Carpenter, Pan, and Butler's 2022 review likewise examines evidence for spacing and retrieval practice and their implications for metacognition. These findings make retrieval and spaced review important candidates for curriculum and study design, but they do not establish one perfect schedule for every learner, subject, or goal. [Dunlosky et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266); [Carpenter et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1038/s44159-022-00089-1).

A 2025 systematic review of classroom-applied distributed practice found a moderate average effect in favor of distributed over massed practice in the included studies (d = 0.54; 95% CI 0.31–0.77). However, it included 22 reports and 31 effect sizes, and the authors noted limits to moderator analysis and long-term follow-up. I will treat the pooled estimate as evidence for a general pattern in the reviewed settings—not as a guaranteed effect size for Bangladeshi schools. [Classroom distributed-practice review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12189222/).

Retrieval practice should also not be equated with frequent high-stakes testing. Low-stakes attempts to recall information with corrective feedback can serve a different function from examinations that determine promotion, status, or admission. The instructional value of retrieval does not, by itself, justify every form of testing.

## 5. Practice, feedback, explanation, and teaching

Practice is not automatically productive merely because it is repeated. Its value depends on what is practised, how difficult it is, whether errors are corrected, whether the learner can understand feedback, and whether the practice matches the desired outcome. Feedback can be unhelpful when it is vague, delayed beyond usefulness, focused only on personal judgement, or gives answers without helping the learner understand the next step.

The review by Weinstein, Madan, and Sumeracki discusses strategies including spaced practice, interleaving, retrieval practice, elaboration, concrete examples, and dual coding. These strategies are tools with boundary conditions, not a checklist that every lesson must follow. [Weinstein et al., 2018](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s41235-017-0087-y).

A meta-analysis of undergraduate STEM studies found that active learning improved examination performance and that failure rates were higher under traditional lecturing than under active learning in the included comparisons. This is meaningful evidence against relying exclusively on uninterrupted lecture in those settings. It does not establish that every activity labelled “active learning” is effective, or that the same numerical effects apply to Bangladeshi schoolchildren, every subject, or every classroom condition. [Freeman et al., 2014](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1319030111).

## 6. Learning is cognitive, social, emotional, and contextual

A learning strategy operates inside a real environment. Motivation, emotional safety, relationships, language, cultural practices, access to materials, teacher expertise, time, and opportunity to learn can change what is possible. The National Academies report warns against treating learners as interchangeable and stresses the interaction between learners' prior experiences and their educational settings. [National Academies, 2018](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/24783/chapter/4).

For Bangladesh, this means that a finding from a well-resourced university, laboratory, or English-language study should not be transferred directly to a crowded secondary classroom without asking whether the relevant mechanisms and conditions are present. Local language, textbook structure, class size, teacher workload, exam incentives, household resources, and tutoring access may affect both implementation and outcomes. These are questions for evidence collection, not assumptions about all Bangladeshi classrooms.

## 7. What this does—and does not—imply for education design

The provisional implication is that education should be designed around specified learning outcomes, adequate prior knowledge, opportunities to practise and retrieve, useful feedback, durable retention, understanding, and transfer where transfer is a goal. It should also account for development, context, and differences among learners.

This does **not** yet establish that one pedagogy should replace all others, that lectures are always ineffective, that discovery learning is always better, that memorization is inherently bad, or that examinations should be eliminated. Foundational knowledge and fluent recall may support complex reasoning; the real question is what should be learned, how deeply, for what purposes, and under which conditions.

## 8. Main uncertainties and next research tasks

1. Search for Bangladesh-specific empirical research on memory, retrieval practice, feedback, active learning, prior knowledge, and transfer.
2. Examine primary studies behind major review conclusions and record population, subject, intervention, comparison, outcome, delay, and risk-of-bias concerns.
3. Investigate whether effects differ by age, prior knowledge, language, disability, subject domain, and assessment stakes.
4. Separate recall, comprehension, problem solving, transfer, practical competence, and long-term retention.
5. Search for credible null or negative results, implementation failures, and conditions where strategies do not help.
6. Examine culturally and linguistically relevant learning research rather than treating English-language evidence as automatically universal.
7. Continue distinguishing the evidence for a learning mechanism from the policy or curriculum choices that someone might make based on it.

## Provisional conclusion

Reliable learning research supports several useful principles, including the importance of prior knowledge, context, retrieval, spacing, feedback, and metacognitive support. But their effects depend on the goal, learner, subject, implementation, and environment. I should build the education-system design from those qualified findings—not from slogans about a single “best” teaching method.
