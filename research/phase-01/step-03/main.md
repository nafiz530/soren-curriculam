# Step 3 — What Is Education For?

**Author:** Md. Nafiz  
**Phase:** 1 — Research Foundation  
**Status:** Draft — review pending  
**Official research title:** *Is our education system actually preparing students for the future, or is it mainly preparing students to pass examinations?*

> This chapter maps major arguments about the purposes of education. It does not assume that one purpose should dominate all others, and it is not an approved design philosophy for the proposed system.

## 1. Why this question comes before system design

If I design curriculum, teaching, assessment, or school structures before examining what education is for, I risk treating familiar arrangements as their own justification. A system might be efficient at producing examination results yet leave other important questions unanswered. Conversely, a system may advertise creativity, well-being, or practical capability without demonstrating that learners acquire the knowledge and understanding needed to exercise them.

The question is not simply whether examinations are good or bad. It is whether the goals, activities, and measures of an education system are aligned—and whether the goals themselves are defensible.

Two kinds of questions must not be confused:

- **Normative:** What should education serve, and why are those purposes worth pursuing?
- **Empirical:** What do particular educational arrangements achieve, for whom, under which conditions, and at what cost?

Evidence can test assumptions about the second question and inform the first. It cannot by itself settle every value disagreement. A measurable outcome is not automatically the most important outcome, and a worthy aim is not proof that a chosen policy will achieve it.

## 2. More than one kind of value

A useful starting distinction is between **intrinsic or constitutive value** and **instrumental value**. The distinction is analytical rather than absolute: an outcome can be valuable in itself and also help produce other benefits.

- **Intrinsic or constitutive value:** learning may enrich understanding, curiosity, reflection, appreciation, or participation in worthwhile intellectual and cultural practices, even when it does not immediately produce a wage or credential.
- **Instrumental value:** education may help people gain employment, make informed decisions, participate in public life, communicate, and continue learning.
- **Relational and collective value:** education can shape how people understand and treat one another, participate in shared institutions, sustain cultural life, and respond to common problems.

It would be a mistake to conclude that intrinsic value is always more important than instrumental value, or the reverse. A learner who needs a reliable livelihood has a serious reason to value employment-related education. A society also has reasons to care about shared knowledge, civic participation, and the ability to examine claims critically. The research must ask how these purposes can coexist and where their demands conflict.

The *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* review of philosophy of education describes longstanding debate over multiple educational goods, including economic productivity, personal autonomy, democratic competence, healthy relationships, equality, and personal fulfilment. This supports treating educational aims as a contested field requiring justification, rather than assuming that one slogan captures them all.

**Source:** [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy — Philosophy of Education](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/education-philosophy/).

## 3. Major purposes to examine

The following are working categories. They overlap, can conflict, and are not a ranked list.

### 3.1 Knowledge, understanding, and intellectual development

Education can give learners access to knowledge they would not reliably acquire by themselves, introduce them to disciplines and ways of investigating the world, and help them distinguish strong reasons from weak ones. Knowledge is not merely a store of facts to reproduce. It can provide foundations needed to explain, question, create, and judge.

The opposite error is to dismiss factual knowledge in favour of generic “skills.” Reasoning about biology, history, mathematics, literature, or public issues depends in part on knowing something about the subject. A later step must investigate how knowledge, practice, and transfer relate; this step should not prematurely prescribe a curriculum.

**Question:** What knowledge is worth making broadly accessible, and how can learners develop understanding rather than only short-term recall?

### 3.2 Personal development, agency, and judgement

Education may help people understand options, form judgements, reflect on reasons, pursue interests, and take increasing responsibility for decisions. Agency should not mean leaving children to teach themselves or treating every preference as equally informed. Meaningful choice often depends on foundational knowledge, guidance, safety, and access to real alternatives.

There is also a legitimate debate about how much an education system should shape learners' values or preferred ways of life. An aim can become paternalistic if authorities assume they alone know what a good life should look like. Yet avoiding all values is not neutral: choices about curriculum and institutional rules already express priorities.

**Question:** How can education support growing agency while respecting learners' rights, developmental needs, family contexts, and legitimate pluralism?

### 3.3 Livelihood, work, and economic participation

Education can help people prepare for employment, develop occupational knowledge, adapt as work changes, and participate in economic life with greater independence. These are substantive purposes, not distractions from “real education.” Where families face financial insecurity, the ability to earn a living can directly affect freedom and well-being.

Yet employment cannot safely become the only measure of educational value. Labour markets change, employers' immediate needs do not exhaust society's needs, and education is not responsible for every economic outcome. Job availability, discrimination, wages, regional opportunity, and economic policy also matter. A qualification or training programme may be useful without guaranteeing employment.

A job-focused system also faces a time-horizon problem: narrowly preparing learners for current tasks may leave them less prepared for unfamiliar tasks later. This is a risk to investigate, not proof that occupational specialization is inherently wrong.

**Question:** How can education support work and economic independence without reducing people to future workers or pretending schools control the labour market?

### 3.4 Citizenship, public reasoning, and shared life

Education may prepare people to understand public institutions, evaluate information, discuss disagreements, cooperate, and participate in collective decisions. These aims do not require every learner to adopt one political opinion. They raise questions about how a society can support informed participation while protecting freedom of thought and legitimate disagreement.

Citizenship education can become indoctrination, a demand for unquestioning loyalty, or a way to exclude minority experiences. Conversely, avoiding all discussion of public life may leave learners less able to understand decisions that affect them.

**Question:** What knowledge and capabilities enable people to participate in society, challenge power responsibly, and disagree without dehumanizing one another?

### 3.5 Culture, identity, and intergenerational transmission

Education can transmit languages, histories, arts, scientific traditions, cultural practices, and accumulated knowledge. Understanding inherited traditions can help people make sense of themselves and others.

Transmission, however, is not unquestioning preservation. Traditions can contain insight and beauty, but also exclusion, injustice, or claims that deserve scrutiny. A defensible approach asks whose knowledge is represented, whose accounts are omitted, and whether learners can examine what they inherit.

This matters in Bangladesh, where language, history, cultural life, and national context are relevant to educational purposes. The research should not assume that a single national narrative represents every learner's experience.

**Question:** How can education sustain valuable cultural knowledge while enabling critical examination, inclusion, and exchange across differences?

### 3.6 Equity, dignity, and human rights

A system's purpose cannot be evaluated only by the average performance of those who already have resources. It must also ask who can participate, who is excluded, whether learners are treated with dignity, and whether opportunities are meaningful rather than merely formal.

Equal treatment and equitable support are not always identical. Learners may face different barriers related to income, disability, language, location, safety, prior opportunity, or access to learning resources. A system may need different forms of support to make meaningful participation possible. The details require evidence and careful design later.

A rights-based justification sets important boundaries for educational policy, but it does not automatically settle every question about curriculum or assessment.

**Question:** What minimum entitlements and protections should every learner have, and how can a system identify unequal outcomes without blaming learners for structural barriers?

### 3.7 Well-being, relationships, and flourishing

Education may contribute to intellectual, physical, emotional, social, and creative development. Relationships with teachers and peers can influence whether a learner feels safe, challenged, respected, or excluded. Well-being is relevant both to learning conditions and to the broader question of living well.

But “well-being” can become an empty label if it is not defined. It can also be misused to avoid academic challenge or impose one official definition of a successful life. The aim should not be to promise constant happiness. Learning can involve effort, frustration, disagreement, and revision.

**Question:** Which dimensions of well-being are legitimately educational concerns, how might they be supported, and what should remain outside an institution's control?

### 3.8 Collective problem-solving and future generations

Education can help people understand shared challenges, cooperate across differences, and consider long-term consequences. UNESCO's 2021 report *Reimagining our futures together* frames education as part of a renewed social contract and emphasizes education throughout life and education as a public and common good. This is a normative framework for imagining collective futures, not experimental proof that a specific model will achieve them.

The OECD Learning Compass 2030 similarly offers an aspirational framework centred on individual and collective well-being, student agency, and knowledge, skills, attitudes, and values. The OECD describes it as globally informed but intended for local contextualisation. Its breadth is useful for mapping possible aims; it is not a universally binding ranking or proof that every element has equal priority in every setting.

**Sources:** [UNESCO — Reimagining our futures together](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/reimagining-our-futures-together-new-social-contract-education); [OECD — Learning Compass 2030](https://www.oecd.org/en/data/tools/oecd-learning-compass-2030.html).

## 4. What policy documents can—and cannot—tell us

Bangladesh's *National Education Policy 2010* is relevant because it records officially stated national educational aims and commitments. It should be examined directly to understand the values and objectives articulated in the policy. But a policy statement is not evidence that those aims have been achieved in practice. To assess implementation or outcomes, I will need separate evidence about access, teaching, learning, resources, assessment, and the experiences of different groups of learners.

UNICEF's *Education Strategy 2026: Shaping the Future of Learning* frames its current global priorities around learning, equity, and skills development for children and adolescents amid technological, social, and environmental change. It is a strategy document: useful for understanding an institution's priorities, but not neutral proof that every proposed priority is equally important or that a particular intervention works.

**Sources:** Bangladesh Ministry of Education, *National Education Policy 2010* (archived in this repository as SRC-007); [UNICEF — Education Strategy: Shaping the Future of Learning](https://www.unicef.org/reports/education-strategy).

## 5. Why aims can conflict

Educational purposes are not always mutually reinforcing. Several tensions deserve explicit treatment rather than being hidden behind “holistic education.”

| Tension | Why it matters | Question for later research |
|---|---|---|
| Depth vs breadth | Time spent mastering one domain is unavailable for others. | What deserves common coverage, and what can be optional? |
| Common foundations vs learner choice | Shared knowledge can support equality, while excessive uniformity can restrict interests and pathways. | What should everyone learn, and when should choice expand? |
| Work preparation vs broader development | Immediate occupational needs may not align with long-term adaptability, civic life, or intellectual interests. | How should the system balance present needs and future uncertainty? |
| Cultural continuity vs criticism | Learners need cultural knowledge but freedom to examine inherited ideas. | How can transmission avoid becoming indoctrination? |
| Standards vs equity | Common expectations clarify goals, but identical treatment can ignore unequal starting conditions. | What does fair opportunity require? |
| Measurement vs educational value | Some outcomes are easier to count than others. | How can measures inform decisions without becoming the sole purpose? |
| Individual benefit vs collective responsibility | Learners have personal goals, while education affects communities and future generations. | How should legitimate conflicts be resolved? |
| Guidance vs autonomy | Learners need support, but over-control can weaken agency. | How should responsibility change with age and experience? |

No table can resolve these tensions by itself. The point is to make them visible so future design decisions state their reasoning and costs.

## 6. A caution about “future readiness”

Preparing learners for the future matters, but the future is uncertain. A system should not pretend to know exactly which jobs, technologies, or social conditions will exist decades from now. Nor should uncertainty justify abandoning useful preparation.

A more defensible research question is whether education develops a strong foundation for learning, judgement, adaptation, participation, and meaningful work while providing relevant knowledge for present circumstances. This is a hypothesis about a promising direction, not yet an approved design principle. Later research must test it against evidence, feasibility, and competing arguments.

The same caution applies to labels such as “critical thinking,” “creativity,” and “life skills.” They are too broad to guide curriculum unless the research specifies what learners need to know and do, how those capabilities develop, and how development can be supported and assessed fairly.

## 7. A provisional way to evaluate educational purposes

At this stage, I will use the following questions to examine proposed aims. They are review criteria, not a final hierarchy.

1. **Justification:** Why is this purpose worth pursuing? Is the justification ethical, educational, social, economic, or a combination?
2. **Beneficiaries:** Who benefits, and who might bear costs or be excluded?
3. **Knowledge basis:** What knowledge and understanding does the aim depend on?
4. **Evidence:** What is known about whether educational arrangements can support it? What remains uncertain?
5. **Time horizon:** Does the purpose address immediate needs, later life, or both?
6. **Rights and dignity:** Could pursuing it violate learner rights, suppress legitimate difference, or treat learners merely as instruments?
7. **Trade-offs:** What other purposes might receive less time, attention, or resources?
8. **Context:** Does the reasoning fit Bangladesh's circumstances, and which international lessons may not transfer?
9. **Measurement:** What indicators could help, and what important aspects might those indicators miss?
10. **Failure modes:** How could this purpose be distorted, gamed, or used to justify harmful practices?

These criteria aim to prevent two opposite failures: accepting every declared aim without scrutiny, and rejecting a valuable aim because it is difficult to measure.

## 8. Current interpretation

The sources reviewed so far support a cautious conclusion: educational aims are plural and contested, and credible frameworks describe combinations of individual, social, civic, economic, cultural, and lifelong purposes. They do not establish one universally agreed ordering among all these purposes.

My interpretation is that the research should not begin by choosing a single dominant aim. It should identify purposes that can be justified, distinguish minimum rights and protections from goals that require balancing, and examine trade-offs openly. This remains provisional. A wider scholarly review and more Bangladesh-specific evidence may change it.

The current source base also has a limitation: it relies substantially on institutional frameworks and a philosophical overview. These sources help map aims, but the next review should trace major arguments to scholarly works where possible and add evidence about how educational purposes relate to actual learner and system outcomes.
