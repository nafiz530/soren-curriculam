# Step 3 — What Is Education For?

**Author:** Md. Nafiz  
**Phase:** 1 — Research Foundation  
**Status:** Approved (09-10-2026)  
**Official research title:** *Is our education system actually preparing students for the future, or is it mainly preparing students to pass examinations?*

> This chapter maps major arguments about the purposes of education. It does not assume that one purpose should dominate all others, and it is not an approved design philosophy for the proposed system.

## 1. Why this question comes before system design

If I design curriculum, teaching, assessment, or school structures before examining what education is for, I risk treating familiar arrangements as their own justification. A system might be efficient at producing examination results yet leave other important questions unanswered. Conversely, a system may advertise creativity, well-being, or practical capability without demonstrating that learners acquire the knowledge and understanding needed to exercise them.

The question is not simply whether examinations are good or bad. It is whether the goals, activities, and measures of an education system are aligned—and whether the goals themselves are defensible. Alignment alone is not a moral test: a system designed solely to maximize examination scores could align teaching and measurement perfectly while still pursuing too narrow a purpose. We must examine both effectiveness and the justification of the ends.

Two kinds of questions must not be confused:

- **Normative:** What should education serve, and why are those purposes worth pursuing?
- **Empirical:** What do particular educational arrangements achieve, for whom, under which conditions, and at what cost?

Evidence can test assumptions about the second question and inform the first. It cannot by itself settle every value disagreement. A measurable outcome is not automatically the most important outcome, and a worthy aim is not proof that a chosen policy will achieve it.

## 2. More than one kind of value

A useful starting distinction is between **intrinsic or constitutive value** and **instrumental value**. The distinction is analytical rather than absolute: an outcome can be valuable in itself and also help produce other benefits.

- **Intrinsic value:** learning may enrich understanding, curiosity, reflection, appreciation, or participation in worthwhile intellectual and cultural practices, even when it does not immediately produce a wage or credential. **Constitutive value** is related but not identical: an activity or capability may be part of what a flourishing life consists in, rather than merely a tool for reaching a separate outcome. I will not treat the terms as interchangeable without argument.
- **Instrumental value:** education may help people gain employment, make informed decisions, participate in public life, communicate, and continue learning.
- **Relational value:** education can shape how people understand, treat, trust, and cooperate with other people. **Collective value:** education can support shared institutions, public goods, cultural life, and responses to common problems. These overlap but are not identical: a relationship is not the same thing as a society-wide outcome.

It would be a mistake to conclude that intrinsic value is always more important than instrumental value, or the reverse. A learner who needs a reliable livelihood has a serious reason to value employment-related education. A society also has reasons to care about shared knowledge, civic participation, and the ability to examine claims critically. The research must ask how these purposes can coexist and where their demands conflict.

The *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* review of philosophy of education describes longstanding debate over multiple educational goods, including economic productivity, personal autonomy, democratic competence, healthy relationships, equality, and personal fulfilment. This supports treating educational aims as a contested field requiring justification, rather than assuming that one slogan captures them all.

**Source:** [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy — Philosophy of Education](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/education-philosophy/).

## 3. Major purposes to examine

The following are working categories. They overlap, can conflict, and are not a ranked list.

### 3.1 Knowledge, understanding, and intellectual development

Education can give learners access to knowledge they would not reliably acquire by themselves, introduce them to disciplines and ways of investigating the world, and help them distinguish strong reasons from weak ones. Knowledge is not merely a store of facts to reproduce. It can provide foundations needed to explain, question, create, and judge.

The opposite error is to dismiss factual knowledge in favour of generic “skills.” Reasoning about biology, history, mathematics, literature, or public issues depends in part on knowing something about the subject. A later step must investigate how knowledge, practice, and transfer relate; this step should not prematurely prescribe a curriculum.

**Question:** What knowledge is worth making broadly accessible, and how can learners develop understanding rather than only short-term recall?

### 3.2 Personal development, agency, and judgement

Education may help people understand options, form judgements, reflect on reasons, pursue interests, and take increasing responsibility for decisions. Agency should not mean leaving children to teach themselves or treating every preference as equally informed. Meaningful choice often depends on foundational knowledge, guidance, safety, and access to real alternatives.

There is also a legitimate debate about how much an education system should shape learners' values or preferred ways of life. An aim can become paternalistic if authorities assume they alone know what a good life should look like. Yet avoiding all values is not neutral: choices about curriculum and institutional rules already express priorities.

**Question:** How can education support growing agency while respecting learners' rights, developmental needs, family contexts, and legitimate pluralism?

### 3.3 Livelihood, work, and economic participation

Education can help people prepare for employment, develop occupational knowledge, adapt as work changes, and participate in economic life with greater independence. These are substantive purposes, not distractions from “real education.” Where families face financial insecurity, the ability to earn a living can directly affect freedom and well-being.

Yet employment cannot safely become the only measure of educational value. Labour markets change, employers' immediate needs do not exhaust society's needs, and education is not responsible for every economic outcome. Job availability, discrimination, wages, regional opportunity, and economic policy also matter. A qualification or training programme may be useful without guaranteeing employment.

A job-focused system also faces a time-horizon problem: narrowly preparing learners for current tasks may leave them less prepared for unfamiliar tasks later. This is a risk to investigate, not proof that occupational specialization is inherently wrong.

**Question:** How can education support work and economic independence without reducing people to future workers or pretending schools control the labour market?

### 3.4 Citizenship, public reasoning, and shared life

Education may prepare people to understand public institutions, evaluate information, discuss disagreements, cooperate, and participate in collective decisions. These aims do not require every learner to adopt one political opinion. They raise questions about how a society can support informed participation while protecting freedom of thought and legitimate disagreement.

Citizenship education can become indoctrination, a demand for unquestioning loyalty, or a way to exclude minority experiences. Conversely, avoiding all discussion of public life may leave learners less able to understand decisions that affect them.

**Question:** What knowledge and capabilities enable people to participate in society, challenge power responsibly, and disagree without dehumanizing one another?

### 3.5 Culture, identity, and intergenerational transmission

Education can transmit languages, histories, arts, scientific traditions, cultural practices, and accumulated knowledge. Understanding inherited traditions can help people make sense of themselves and others.

Transmission, however, is not unquestioning preservation. Traditions can contain insight and beauty, but also exclusion, injustice, or claims that deserve scrutiny. A defensible approach asks whose knowledge is represented, whose accounts are omitted, and whether learners can examine what they inherit.

This matters in Bangladesh, where language, history, cultural life, and national context are relevant to educational purposes. The research should not assume that a single national narrative represents every learner's experience.

**Question:** How can education sustain valuable cultural knowledge while enabling critical examination, inclusion, and exchange across differences?

### 3.6 Equity, dignity, and human rights

A system's purpose cannot be evaluated only by the average performance of those who already have resources. It must also ask who can participate, who is excluded, whether learners are treated with dignity, and whether opportunities are meaningful rather than merely formal.

Equal treatment and equitable support are not always identical. Learners may face different barriers related to income, disability, language, location, safety, prior opportunity, or access to learning resources. A system may need different forms of support to make meaningful participation possible. The details require evidence and careful design later.

A rights-based justification sets important boundaries for educational policy, but it does not automatically settle every question about curriculum or assessment.

Amartya Sen's capability approach offers a useful way to deepen this question: formal access to a resource does not guarantee a person's real opportunity to use it. The relevant inquiry includes what learners are actually able to do and become, given personal, social, and institutional conditions. This is a framework for analysis, not a shortcut to a ready-made list of capabilities for Bangladesh. **Question:** What minimum entitlements and protections should every learner have, and how can a system identify unequal outcomes without blaming learners for structural barriers?

### 3.7 Well-being, relationships, and flourishing

Education may contribute to intellectual, physical, emotional, social, and creative development. Relationships with teachers and peers can influence whether a learner feels safe, challenged, respected, or excluded. Well-being is relevant both to learning conditions and to the broader question of living well.

But “well-being” can become an empty label if it is not defined. It can also be misused to avoid academic challenge or impose one official definition of a successful life. The aim should not be to promise constant happiness. Learning can involve effort, frustration, disagreement, and revision.

**Question:** Which dimensions of well-being are legitimately educational concerns, how might they be supported, and what should remain outside an institution's control?

### 3.8 Collective problem-solving and future generations

Education can help people understand shared challenges, cooperate across differences, and consider long-term consequences. UNESCO's 2021 report *Reimagining our futures together* frames education as part of a renewed social contract and emphasizes education throughout life and education as a public and common good. This is a normative framework for imagining collective futures, not experimental proof that a specific model will achieve them.

The OECD Learning Compass 2030 similarly offers an aspirational framework centred on individual and collective well-being, student agency, and knowledge, skills, attitudes, and values. The OECD describes it as globally informed but intended for local contextualisation. Its breadth is useful for mapping possible aims; it is not a universally binding ranking or proof that every element has equal priority in every setting. **Climate and ecological understanding must be named explicitly**, rather than hidden inside a general phrase such as “collective problem-solving.” For Bangladesh, climate exposure, disaster preparedness, adaptation, environmental health, and the ethics of intergenerational responsibility are relevant questions; their precise curricular place requires evidence and later design work.

**Sources:** [UNESCO — Reimagining our futures together](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/reimagining-our-futures-together-new-social-contract-education); [OECD — Learning Compass 2030](https://www.oecd.org/en/data/tools/oecd-learning-compass-2030.html).


### 3.9 Certification, selection, and signaling

Education systems do not only teach and develop learners. They also **certify** what a learner has achieved, **select** among applicants when places or opportunities are limited, and **signal** information to universities, employers, families, and the wider public. These are distinct functions:

- **Certification:** a credible claim that a learner has met a defined standard or demonstrated a capability.
- **Selection and allocation:** decisions about access to scarce places, scholarships, programmes, or jobs.
- **Signaling:** a qualification or result provides information to a decision-maker who cannot directly observe every applicant's knowledge, effort, or future performance.

Michael Spence's 1973 model of job-market signaling helps explain why educational credentials can matter even when employers cannot directly observe productivity. It does not imply that every credential is a valid measure of competence, or that credentials only signal rather than teach. Ronald Dore's *The Diploma Disease* (1976) examines how competition for scarce jobs can turn schooling into a ritualized race for qualifications, including cases in South Asia. Dore's diagnosis is a major hypothesis to investigate in Bangladesh, not proof that every Bangladeshi examination has the same effects. Randall Collins's work on credentialism and Fred Hirsch's analysis of positional goods also help explain why families may pursue relative advantage through credentials even if all families would prefer less damaging competition.

This produces an important system constraint: **a reform cannot responsibly remove examination pressure without explaining how trustworthy certification and fair allocation would work instead**. It may be possible to improve what is assessed, diversify evidence, reduce unnecessary stakes, or separate learning feedback from selection decisions. But alternative methods also have risks: portfolios can be costly to verify; teacher assessments can vary; practical tests need resources; and interviews or recommendations can magnify social bias. The task is not to assume exams are indispensable or obsolete, but to compare their validity, fairness, cost, transparency, and susceptibility to gaming against credible alternatives.

The pressure is partly positional. If university seats, prestigious schools, scholarships, or secure jobs are scarce, a family can rationally seek a higher rank even when exam-focused preparation narrows learning for everyone. This can create an arms race: individual strategies that appear rational produce a collective outcome that many participants dislike. Therefore, changing classroom methods alone may not reduce exam orientation if selection incentives remain unchanged.

**Sources:** [Spence, “Job Market Signaling” (1973)](https://doi.org/10.2307/1882010); [Dore, *The Diploma Disease* (1976)](https://books.google.com/books/about/The_Diploma_Disease.html?id=FOMf7FDKEMwC); [ERIC record for Dore's book](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED153584).

## 4. Bangladesh: stated aims, reform history, and continuity

Bangladesh must be treated as a lived institutional context, not simply an example attached to international frameworks. The country's educational landscape includes Bangla-medium, English-version, English-medium, general madrasa, and technical/vocational pathways, alongside major differences in geography, household resources, school quality, and access to private coaching. These pathways are not interchangeable, and broad labels can conceal substantial variation within each. The purposes of education must be examined in relation to this diversity rather than assuming one learner experience.

### 4.1 What the National Education Policy 2010 says

The *National Education Policy 2010* remains a central primary document for understanding Bangladesh's formally stated national educational aims. It is the latest fully adopted national education policy identified in the sources reviewed for this draft; however, later public statements about scrapping it make its current administrative status a separate question that requires primary documentary confirmation. I will treat the policy as evidence of declared aims, not as proof that it was fully implemented or that every provision remains operative in the same form today. The table below is an **analytical mapping of broad themes**, not a quotation or claim that the policy uses these exact categories. Detailed wording should be checked against the archived policy document before any legal or historical conclusion depends on it.

| Broad stated aim or theme to verify in the primary policy | Step 3 category it relates to | Research caution |
|---|---|---|
| Humanistic, moral, ethical, patriotic, and socially responsible development | Personal development; citizenship; culture and values | Moral and civic formation can support responsibility, but can also be used to demand conformity. The meaning of these terms must be examined. |
| Scientific outlook, knowledge, creativity, and quality learning | Knowledge and understanding; future capabilities | Policy language does not show whether learners consistently gain these outcomes. |
| Skills, productivity, technical competence, and employment relevance | Livelihood and economic participation | Job relevance matters, but labour-market outcomes depend on factors beyond schools. |
| Equal opportunity, reducing discrimination, and widening access | Equity and human rights | Formal commitments must be compared with access, participation, and outcome data. |
| Language, national history, culture, and heritage | Culture and intergenerational transmission | Historical and cultural content should be evidence-led and open to scholarly scrutiny, not treated as a party's property. |
| Religious and moral education within the national context | Values, identity, and pluralism | The policy's exact provisions, and how they interact with religious diversity and learner rights, need careful reading. |

The point of this mapping is not to declare the 2010 policy correct or incorrect. It is to make its stated purposes visible and compare them with later curricular decisions, implementation evidence, and learners' actual experiences. It also guards against claiming that a new policy is needed simply because an older one belongs to a previous political period.

### 4.2 Policy, curriculum, and implementation are different things

A national education policy, a curriculum framework, a syllabus, a textbook, an assessment rule, and a political announcement are different kinds of instruments. They may change on different timelines and have different legal or administrative status. For this reason, I will not describe every curriculum revision as a replacement of the national education policy.

A provisional chronology to verify through primary documents is:

- **2010:** The National Education Policy was adopted. Its declared aims are a baseline for examining continuity and implementation.
- **2012:** NCTB curriculum revision and related curriculum changes need to be traced to the relevant official documents; their scope and relationship to the 2010 policy should not be inferred from the year alone.
- **2021–2022:** A new national curriculum framework was developed and announced. Framework publication is not the same as full nationwide implementation.
- **2023 onward:** Implementation began progressively for selected grades, including Grades 1, 6, and 7, with further expansion planned. The actual grade-by-grade timeline and assessment changes must be documented using NCTB and Ministry records.
- **2024–2026:** Curriculum and assessment arrangements were revised again. NCTB's official pages list a “National Curriculum 2021 - Primary Level (Revised 2025)” and revised curricula/assessment guidance for 2026. Separate reform bodies also worked on different parts of the system: the Primary and Non-Formal Education Reform Advisory Committee submitted recommendations in February 2025, and a Review Committee for the Secondary and Higher Secondary Education System published a vision report in February 2026. These are substantive reform processes, but they are not necessarily the same thing as a single, comprehensive national education reform commission.

**Current-status caution (checked for this revision on 9 October 2026):** Public reporting in December 2024 said the interim government intended to scrap the 2010 policy and announce an education reform commission. A report dated 22 January 2025 quoted the education adviser saying there was no plan to form such a commission at that time; reporting in August 2025 also said a broad education-sector reform commission had not been constituted. However, this does **not** mean the interim government formed no education-reform bodies. A Primary and Non-Formal Education Reform Advisory Committee submitted recommendations in February 2025, and the Ministry of Education's Secondary and Higher Education Division published a February 2026 report from a review committee on transforming the secondary and higher-secondary system. The defensible distinction is between specialized committees/review bodies and a single comprehensive education reform commission.

A further political transition has occurred since the interim period. In 2026, government representatives publicly described work on a new primary-education policy and plans for a major curriculum redesign for the 2028 academic session. These are reported plans, not proof that a replacement national education policy has already been adopted. The current government’s proposals must be evaluated by the same standard as earlier reforms: public rationale, evidence, consultation, historical integrity, and implementation outcomes—not by assuming that continuity is always right or that change is automatically partisan.

### 4.3 Educational continuity across political transitions

I consider one principle important enough to state provisionally: **education's historical record and core educational commitments should not be rewritten merely to signal the arrival of a new political party or government**. This is a normative commitment for the research to examine, not a claim that educational policy must never change.

Three distinctions matter:

1. **Evidence-based revision versus partisan reversal.** New research, documented implementation failures, changing social needs, scientific developments, and legitimate public deliberation can justify reform. A change justified mainly by partisan ownership, retaliation, or political branding is a different matter.
2. **Historical accuracy versus political convenience.** Teaching history necessarily involves selection and interpretation, but claims should be accountable to evidence, transparent about uncertainty, and open to qualified scholarly debate. No party should be able to make a preferred political narrative immune from evidence or erase inconvenient events merely because it has taken office.
3. **Stable foundations versus frozen policy.** Long-term aims, constitutional and human-rights commitments, historical integrity, and the basic entitlement to education need continuity. Specific textbooks, teaching methods, syllabi, assessment arrangements, and implementation plans may need revision when evidence supports it.

A possible safeguard is a transparent curriculum-review process with published reasons for changes, independent subject expertise, consultation across political and social groups, clear version histories, publicly accessible evidence, protections for academic freedom, and a distinction between correcting factual errors and changing contested interpretations. This is a **proposed governance principle**, not an established finding that any one procedure will be sufficient. Later research must compare governance models and examine risks such as expert capture, partisan appointments, elite exclusion, and slow responses to genuine problems.

This principle should apply across political parties, not selectively to any one party. Historical memory and education belong to society across generations; they should not become disposable instruments of electoral competition. At the same time, continuity must not become an excuse to preserve false claims, discriminatory practices, or ineffective policy. The test is whether change is justified by public reasons and credible evidence, not simply whether it preserves or reverses a previous government's choices.

### 4.4 What this chapter cannot yet conclude

The current evidence is not sufficient to judge the full implementation record of the 2010 policy, the outcomes of every curriculum reform, the motives behind each political decision, or the quality of all pathways in Bangladesh. Those questions require official curriculum documents, policy texts, research on classrooms and examinations, learner and teacher evidence, and careful attention to differences among school types and communities. I will not infer partisan intent from chronology alone.

## 5. Why aims can conflict

Educational purposes are not always mutually reinforcing. Several tensions deserve explicit treatment rather than being hidden behind “holistic education.”

| Tension | Why it matters | Question for later research |
|---|---|---|
| Depth vs breadth | Time spent mastering one domain is unavailable for others. | What deserves common coverage, and what can be optional? |
| Common foundations vs learner choice | Shared knowledge can support equality, while excessive uniformity can restrict interests and pathways. | What should everyone learn, and when should choice expand? |
| Work preparation vs broader development | Immediate occupational needs may not align with long-term adaptability, civic life, or intellectual interests. | How should the system balance present needs and future uncertainty? |
| Cultural continuity vs criticism | Learners need cultural knowledge but freedom to examine inherited ideas. | How can transmission avoid becoming indoctrination? |
| Standards vs equity | Common expectations clarify goals, but identical treatment can ignore unequal starting conditions. | What does fair opportunity require? |
| Measurement vs educational value | Some outcomes are easier to count than others. When a measure becomes a target, people may optimize the indicator rather than the underlying purpose (often discussed through Goodhart's law and Campbell's law). | How can measures inform decisions without becoming the sole purpose, and what independent checks detect gaming? |
| Certification vs learning | Credentials help communicate achievement and allocate opportunities, but high-stakes competition can narrow learning and reward strategic test preparation. | Which functions require common standards, and which could use broader evidence? |
| Continuity vs reform | Stable foundations protect long-term learning and historical integrity, while systems must correct failures and respond to new evidence. | Which commitments need durable cross-party agreement, and how should revisions be justified? |
| Expertise vs democratic authority | Curriculum requires specialist knowledge but also makes public choices about history, values, identity, and opportunity. | Who has authority to decide, who must be consulted, and how are reasons and dissent made visible? |
| Individual benefit vs collective responsibility | Learners have personal goals, while education affects communities and future generations. | How should legitimate conflicts be resolved? |
| Guidance vs autonomy | Learners need support, but over-control can weaken agency. | How should responsibility change with age and experience? |

No table can resolve these tensions by itself. The point is to make them visible so future design decisions state their reasoning and costs.

## 6. A caution about “future readiness”

Preparing learners for the future matters, but the future is uncertain. A system should not pretend to know exactly which jobs, technologies, or social conditions will exist decades from now. Nor should uncertainty justify abandoning useful preparation.

A more defensible research question is whether education develops a strong foundation for learning, judgement, adaptation, participation, and meaningful work while providing relevant knowledge for present circumstances. This is a hypothesis about a promising direction, not yet an approved design principle. Later research must test it against evidence, feasibility, and competing arguments.

The same caution applies to labels such as “critical thinking,” “creativity,” and “life skills.” They are too broad to guide curriculum unless the research specifies what learners need to know and do, how those capabilities develop, and how development can be supported and assessed fairly.

## 7. A provisional way to evaluate educational purposes

At this stage, I will use the following questions to examine proposed aims. They are review criteria, not a final hierarchy.

1. **Justification:** Why is this purpose worth pursuing? Is the justification ethical, educational, social, economic, or a combination?
2. **Beneficiaries:** Who benefits, and who might bear costs or be excluded?
3. **Knowledge basis:** What knowledge and understanding does the aim depend on?
4. **Evidence:** What is known about whether educational arrangements can support it? What remains uncertain?
5. **Time horizon:** Does the purpose address immediate needs, later life, or both?
6. **Rights and dignity:** Could pursuing it violate learner rights, suppress legitimate difference, or treat learners merely as instruments?
7. **Trade-offs:** What other purposes might receive less time, attention, or resources?
8. **Context:** Does the reasoning fit Bangladesh's circumstances, and which international lessons may not transfer?
9. **Measurement:** What indicators could help, and what important aspects might those indicators miss?
10. **Failure modes:** How could this purpose be distorted, gamed, or used to justify harmful practices?

These criteria aim to prevent two opposite failures: accepting every declared aim without scrutiny, and rejecting a valuable aim because it is difficult to measure.

They also need a decision process. I propose that later design decisions identify who has legitimate authority, whose experience must be heard, what evidence is required, how conflicts are weighed, and how the decision can be challenged or revised. Experts should advise on factual and technical questions; democratic institutions and affected communities have legitimate roles in public value choices; learners' rights constrain both. No single actor should be treated as infallible.

For this research, three **provisional commitments** should be tested rather than silently deferred:

1. **Rights and dignity are constraints, not just one benefit to trade away.** Efficiency, prestige, or aggregate scores should not justify violations of basic learner rights or discriminatory exclusion.
2. **Trustworthy certification is a function any proposed system must preserve or replace.** A reform must explain how it will make fair, credible decisions about progression and scarce opportunities.
3. **Educational continuity should be protected from partisan rewriting, while evidence-based revision remains possible.** A change should have a public rationale, traceable evidence, transparent consultation, and a clear record of what changed and why.

These are starting commitments for scrutiny, not a complete moral theory or a final ranking of every educational aim. The precise meaning of rights constraints, certification standards, and cross-party continuity still requires legal, philosophical, empirical, and Bangladesh-specific work.

## 8. Current interpretation

The sources reviewed so far support a cautious conclusion: educational aims are plural and contested, and credible frameworks describe combinations of individual, social, civic, economic, cultural, and lifelong purposes. They do not establish one universally agreed ordering among all these purposes.

My interpretation is that the research should not begin by choosing a single dominant aim. It should identify purposes that can be justified, distinguish minimum rights and protections from goals that require balancing, and examine trade-offs openly. This remains provisional. A wider scholarly review and more Bangladesh-specific evidence may change it.

The initial source base relied too heavily on institutional frameworks and a philosophical overview. This revision adds Ronald Dore on credential competition, Gert Biesta's qualification/socialization/subjectification framework, and Michael Spence's signaling model. These works sharpen the analysis but do not settle it: Biesta's framework is influential and debated, Dore's account must be tested against present-day Bangladesh rather than copied wholesale, and Spence's model explains one function of credentials rather than proving that credentials measure competence. Further work should add Amartya Sen's capability approach, Fred Hirsch on positional goods, Randall Collins on credentialism, and Bangladesh-specific research on coaching and examination washback.
